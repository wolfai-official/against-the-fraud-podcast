Alex: Good morning, and welcome to Against the Fraud Daily. It is Wednesday, November eighteenth, twenty twenty-six. I am Alex.
Jordan: And I am Jordan. Today, the Justice Department announces the first False Claims Act settlement ever against a PACE senior care provider, thirty-six million dollars, brought by two former employees who said the care was not getting delivered. We will explain what this case means for every capitated care program in the country. And in the tech world, Oregon sues OpenAI for more than two billion dollars over ChatGPT and children. Let us get into it.
Alex: Our case of the day comes out of Denver, Colorado, and it is about a program most listeners have never heard of, even though it cares for some of the most vulnerable seniors in America. It is called PACE, the Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly. It is a joint Medicare and Medicaid program for people fifty-five and older who qualify for nursing home level care, but who can live at home with enough support. Instead of paying for each visit or procedure, the government pays the PACE organization a fixed monthly amount for every person enrolled. In return, the organization has to provide all of the comprehensive care that person needs, and provide it as quickly as the person's health requires.
Jordan: On Tuesday, the Justice Department announced that InnovAge Holding Corporation, a Denver-based company that runs PACE centers in California, Colorado, New Mexico, and Virginia, has agreed to pay thirty-six million dollars to resolve allegations that it violated the federal False Claims Act and state false claims laws. About twenty-five point seven million dollars goes to the United States, and about ten point three million dollars goes to the four states combined.
Alex: The government says that from May of twenty sixteen through October of twenty twenty-two, InnovAge failed to provide comprehensive health care services as quickly as its patients required. Specifically, the company allegedly failed to maintain a sufficient network of specialist providers, and in thousands of instances failed to schedule appointments or consultations for specialist care on time. We are talking about oncology, cardiology, surgery, and neurology. For seniors whose health depends on seeing a specialist promptly, a delayed referral is not a paperwork problem. It is a health problem.
Jordan: What makes this case striking is where the evidence came from. The Justice Department says InnovAge's own internal reports regularly showed numerous and recurrent delinquent specialist referrals. Several senior physicians at the company complained to management that the scheduling practices and delays were harming patients, putting legal risk on the company's own doctors, and putting the company out of compliance with the requirements of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services. And the company's own internal audits found significant delays in specialist care, including numerous delayed orders, many of which had been outstanding for more than a year.
Alex: The acting head of the Justice Department's Civil Division, Jonathan Guynn, called this a historic, first-of-its-kind settlement, and said it demonstrates the Civil Division's commitment to combating health care fraud by PACE organizations. The United States attorney for Colorado, Peter McNeilly, said elderly patients suffer when PACE providers do not fulfill their obligations to schedule visits with specialists in a timely manner. And the inspector general's office at Health and Human Services said delays can jeopardize the health of the patients these programs serve.
Jordan: Two whistleblowers brought this case. Monita Poudyal and Karen Lapcewich, both former employees, filed separate qui tam lawsuits in federal court in Denver, one in twenty twenty-one and one in twenty twenty-two. The governments intervened, and the two cases were resolved together. The relators' share of the federal settlement will be four point five million dollars. That is about seventeen and a half percent of the federal recovery, squarely in the usual range for a case where the government intervenes. And remember, these are allegations only. The settlement resolves the claims, and there has been no determination of liability.
Alex: There is important history here, because this settlement did not come out of nowhere. In twenty twenty-one, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services audited InnovAge and found the company was failing to provide medically necessary services. The agency froze new enrollment at the company's Sacramento center in September of twenty twenty-one, and at its Colorado centers that December. Both freezes were eventually lifted, after the company passed validation audits. But the audit findings, the sanctions, and the whistleblower suits were all circling the same problem, and five years later, that problem is now a thirty-six million dollar settlement. If you are an attorney with a client in a capitated program, that timeline is the story.
Jordan: And that brings us to our practice note, because the legal theory in this case is different from the billing fraud we usually cover, and it matters for a whole category of providers. In a fee-for-service case, the government says you billed for something you did not do. Here, the government says something else. It says you collected a fixed monthly payment for a person, and then did not deliver the care that payment was buying. The false claim is not an inflated invoice. It is the monthly payment itself, collected while the care was not provided.
Alex: That theory opens a new front. PACE is a small program, but capitation is everywhere. Managed care plans, Medicare Advantage organizations, and other providers paid a flat amount per patient all face the same logic. If the payment buys comprehensive care, and the care is not delivered, the payment can be a false claim. The evidence will look like the evidence here. Referral backlogs, network adequacy gaps, internal reports showing orders sitting for months, and clinicians warning management in writing. Attorneys advising capitated providers should be asking whether their clients can prove, with data, that referrals are being completed on time. Because in this case, the company's own records did most of the government's work.
Jordan: Two more practice points from this settlement. First, the announcement mentions no corporate integrity agreement. Many large health care settlements come with one, a formal compliance program overseen by the inspector general. This one, as announced, does not. That may reflect the company's earlier corrective action plans under the Medicare sanctions, or it may reflect a lighter touch in current resolutions. Either way, do not assume a settlement of this size automatically carries a corporate integrity agreement. Read the announcement, and read the agreement when it is public.
Alex: Second, the relator math. Two relators, two separate lawsuits, one coordinated settlement, and a single combined share of four point five million dollars. When multiple relators file on the same conduct, the government can resolve the cases together, and the share is negotiated as one number. If you represent a second filer, your client's recovery may depend on how the cases are coordinated, not just on what your client knew. File early, cooperate, and expect the government to treat parallel suits as one resolution.
Jordan: Now the tech angle, and it starts with how this case was proved. There was no dramatic raid, and no single smoking gun document. The proof was the company's own data, accumulated over years. Internal reports tracking delinquent referrals. Internal audits measuring how long specialist orders sat outstanding. A federal audit in twenty twenty-one that reached the same conclusions independently. In capitated care, the fraud is not hidden in a billing code. It is visible in a queue. The question is whether anyone is watching the queue, and here, two employees were.
Alex: That is the same lesson we have seen across this show. In the cyber cases, the fraud was in filed security scores and certifications. In the lab cases, it was in payment records matched against referral data. Here, it is in referral timeliness data, the time between a doctor ordering a specialist visit and the visit actually happening. For attorneys and compliance officers, the takeaway is practical. If your client's system cannot produce a report showing how long referrals take, that is a risk. And if the system can produce that report, and the numbers are bad, that report is discoverable. Build the dashboard before the government builds the case from it.
Jordan: And now the tech whistleblower watch, because yesterday brought a major development on a story we have been following. On Tuesday, Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield sued OpenAI in state court in Multnomah County, seeking at least two point zero three five billion dollars in damages. The complaint asks the court to order stronger safeguards, including effective age checks, protections against harmful interactions, accurate risk disclosures, and reliable parental alerts.
