## Transcript

Alex: Welcome back to Against the Fraud Daily. It is Monday, November sixteenth, twenty twenty-six, and today we have the Securities and Exchange Commission's report card for the year, a new rule that could raise payouts for commodities whistleblowers, and the technology the commission is now using to catch fraud faster. Plus a quick stop on the docket, where a moving company paid to settle claims it bought good reviews from the troops it was moving.

Jordan: And a watch item on OpenAI, where a congressional deadline came and went with no public answer. Alex, start us with the commission's numbers, because they tell a story that is not obvious at first glance.

Alex: On November tenth, the commission released its enforcement results for fiscal twenty twenty-six, which ended September thirtieth. The headline is that the commission filed three hundred and forty-eight enforcement actions, down about twenty-four percent from four hundred and fifty-six the year before. It obtained orders for about one billion ninety-five million dollars in monetary relief, made up of seven hundred and fifty-two million in disgorgement and interest and three hundred and forty-three million in civil penalties. Those totals are well below prior years, and the commission's enforcement director, David Woodcock, did not apologize for it. Speaking the same evening at Fordham Law School, he told the audience that activity and effectiveness are not the same, and that case counts cannot show whether fraud was stopped early or whether money actually reached victims.

Jordan: And on that second measure, the money reaching victims, the year looks very different. The commission returned about seven hundred and thirty-one million dollars to more than two hundred and fifty thousand harmed investors, nearly triple the two hundred and sixty-two million it returned the year before. So fewer cases, smaller headline penalties, but almost three times as much money back in investors' pockets. That is the number the commission's leadership wants you to look at.

Alex: The whistleblower numbers are the ones our listeners will care about most. The commission received about twenty-six thousand whistleblower tips in the fiscal year, and it awarded more than one hundred and fifty-eight million dollars to sixty-five individual whistleblowers. That is the highest number of individual whistleblowers paid since fiscal twenty twenty-three, and a sharp rebound from fiscal twenty twenty-five, when the commission awarded about sixty million dollars to forty-eight people. For comparison, fiscal twenty twenty-four saw about two hundred and fifty-five million dollars in awards.

Jordan: Now, a listener who followed yesterday's episode will ask the obvious question. If the program is paying more people, why is Michael Bacon still fighting in the Third Circuit over an award the commission cut from about one hundred and seventy-nine point five million dollars to fifty-four point five million? The answer is that both things can be true at once. The program can be paying more whistleblowers while one whistleblower argues the commission broke the law by shrinking his award below the ten percent floor Congress wrote into the Dodd-Frank Act. The commission's response in that case is due December twenty-first, and the outcome will matter to every securities whistleblower whose award was calculated the same way.

Alex: One more detail from the results that shows where enforcement is headed. Forty-five of the commission's two hundred and thirty-five standalone actions targeted firms the commission says filed false registration forms to pose as investment advisers. Those cases came in two waves, six entities in November of twenty twenty-five and thirty-nine in August of twenty twenty-six. That is a data sweep, not a tip, and it leads directly into our tech angle later in the show. But first, the practice note, because the commodities regulator just changed the math for its own whistleblowers. Jordan, walk us through it.

Jordan: This is the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, and on September eleventh it approved a final rule that took effect October sixteenth. The rule creates a presumption that a whistleblower whose tip leads to collected sanctions of up to about sixteen point seven million dollars will receive thirty percent of the money collected, the statutory maximum, so long as the resulting award is five million dollars or less. The commission estimates that about eighty-two percent of meritorious awards will now fall under that presumption. The rule is modeled on one the securities commission already uses, and it is meant to make awards faster and more predictable. The commission has said it takes, on average, more than two and a half years to move from a claim to a final award, and that delay discourages people from coming forward.

Alex: Thirty percent as a starting point is a big change for anyone advising a commodities whistleblower, but the presumption has conditions, and that is where the practice note gets practical.

Jordan: Exactly. The presumption does not apply if the whistleblower was culpable in the underlying violations, unreasonably delayed reporting, or interfered with the company's internal compliance and reporting systems. And there is a separate caution from a July decision of the District of Columbia Circuit, in a case called the Estate of Jennions against the commission. There, a former bank employee gave information to Britain's financial regulator about alleged manipulation of foreign exchange benchmark rates. The commission later brought enforcement actions against five banks, but the court upheld the denial of his award, because the investigation had been prompted by a detailed media article, not by his information. So the lesson for attorneys is twofold. First, advise clients that the new presumption is real money, and file early, before the story is public. Second, document exactly what original information the client provided and how it connects to the enforcement action, because if the agency can trace its investigation to another source, the award can still be denied.

Alex: The numbers behind the rule are worth knowing too. Since its first award in twenty fourteen, the commodities commission has paid more than five hundred and eighty million dollars to whistleblowers, tied to more than five point one billion dollars in monetary sanctions. And just three days after approving the new rule, the commission announced ten awards totaling more than one hundred and fifty million dollars, which shows the pipeline was already moving before the presumption took effect.

Jordan: Now the tech angle, and it connects to the securities commission's results we opened with. The commission has created a new Office of Super Intelligence and Analytics, and the enforcement director says its job is to force-multiply the staff's resources. That office works on artificial intelligence enabled anomaly detection, faster review of documents and transcripts, and improved blockchain and financial flow analysis. The point, in the director's words, is not to replace careful human judgment, but to give staff better information sooner.

Alex: This is not the commission's first step into data analytics. For years it has used a system called Artemis to cross-reference trading data against corporate events in insider trading investigations, and risk-based tools to flag potential earnings management. What is new is scale and speed. The commission's Retail Fraud Working Group now uses artificial intelligence and data from multiple regulatory and law enforcement sources to generate investigative leads, and the commission reports that more than a dozen new investigations have already resulted. And remember those forty-five fake adviser cases. Those came from screening registration data, not from a whistleblower walking in the door. The pattern is the same one we see in False Claims Act cases. The fraud is found by matching one dataset against another, and the human whistleblower still matters most when the data cannot see the whole story.

Jordan: The practical warning for companies is in the commission's own alert. If the staff can review a document production with generative tools and spot themes that traditional text analytics miss, then subpoenas and document requests are going to arrive faster, more targeted, and with shorter deadlines. And the commission is still policing overstated claims about artificial intelligence by public companies, so the technology is both a tool of enforcement and a subject of it.

Alex: Quick stop on the docket. On November tenth, the United States Attorney's Office for the Southern District of Illinois announced that Interstate Group Holdings, a Virginia moving company, agreed to pay three hundred and twenty-five thousand dollars to resolve False Claims Act allegations. The company moved household goods for service members under the Defense Personal Property Program, run by the United States Transportation Command at Scott Air Force Base. The government alleged that between August of twenty seventeen and February of twenty twenty-three, the company gave service members improper service recovery payments, inconvenience payments, and modest gifts, some of them for the purpose of influencing customer satisfaction survey scores. Those scores partly decide how many shipments a company gets, so a better score means more business, paid for by the government.

Jordan: And this is a pattern, not a one-off. About a year ago, another company in the same program, Denali Group, agreed to pay three point five million dollars after the government alleged its employees submitted false customer satisfaction surveys while posing as service members. Same program, same score, different trick. The claims in both cases are allegations only, and there has been no determination of liability, but the message to contractors is clear. If the government scores you, the score itself is a fraud risk.

Alex: One watch item before we close. The deadline for OpenAI to answer eleven questions from twenty House Democrats, led by Representative Greg Casar, passed on Friday, November thirteenth. The questions covered the firing of three safety researchers and a series of security incidents involving the company's models. As of this morning, we have still not found a public response. We will keep checking, because a congressional request with no answer is itself a story.

Jordan: And that is the show. The big picture today is that enforcement is getting smaller in count and sharper in focus, the commodities commission just made small whistleblower awards presumptively worth the maximum, and the regulators are now using the same data analytics that have been proving fraud cases for years. If you are advising a whistleblower, the through line is the same. File early, document the original information, and assume the other side's data will be checked against yours.

Alex: Thanks for listening to Against the Fraud Daily. We will be back tomorrow morning with the newest whistleblower and government fraud developments. If you have a tip or a case we should cover, find us at against the fraud dot com.
