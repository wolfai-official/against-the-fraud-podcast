Eylea is Regeneron's treatment for wet age-related macular degeneration.
Jordan: Here is the background. The government alleges Regeneron paid more than sixty million dollars over about five years to a charitable foundation called the Chronic Disease Fund, to subsidize patient copays for Eylea, with the intent of inducing doctors to prescribe the drug and improperly billing Medicare. Regeneron disputes that the payments were kickbacks, and the claims are allegations only, with no determination of liability.
Alex: The fight is over one phrase in the Anti-Kickback Statute, added in twenty ten, which says a claim that includes items or services resulting from a kickback violation counts as a false claim. Judge F. Dennis Saylor ruled that resulting from means but-for causation, and granted Regeneron partial summary judgment. The First Circuit agreed in February of this year, joining the Sixth and Eighth Circuits. The Third Circuit reads the same words differently, requiring only a link between the kickback and the claim.
Jordan: That but-for ruling narrowed the government's case, so in August the court let prosecutors pursue a second theory, false certification. Under that theory, the government has to show Regeneron falsely certified that it complied with the anti-kickback law, and that the certification was material to the government's decision to pay. Thursday's ruling says the relaxed causation standard applies to that theory, so the government does not have to prove but-for causation there.
Alex: And here is why this matters beyond one drug company. Inside the same courthouse, Judge Saylor and Judge Nathaniel Gorton have now reached opposite conclusions on causation, Saylor requiring but-for in Regeneron, Gorton rejecting it in the Teva case. Add the circuit split, and the First Circuit's February decision, and you have a question that is heading toward the Supreme Court.
Jordan: The practical point for attorneys is simple. In a kickback case built on the Anti-Kickback Statute, plead both theories. The causation standard now depends on which theory you are standing on, and the false certification theory may be the route around the but-for wall the First Circuit built. For defense counsel, that second theory is the one to watch, because it changes what the government has to prove at trial.

Jordan: The tool looks for patterns. High volume submissions from the same provider. Boilerplate language repeated across different veterans. Signs a document was altered. And an examiner located more than a hundred miles from the veteran. Those are the fingerprints of what veterans advocates call D B Q mills, companies that pose as legitimate medical providers and file claims on veterans' behalf while charging them excessive fees.
Alex: The reason the department is building it is in the Inspector General's own numbers. In a January twenty twenty-four report, the Inspector General said that of nearly thirty-two thousand claims completed in twenty twenty-two, sixty-nine percent contained one or more indicators of potential fraud risk, with an estimated value of about three hundred ninety million dollars. Over the past ten years, the department has sent cease and desist letters to at least forty companies in this space.
Jordan: The department has also set a guardrail that matters. Officials say the tool will not be used to go after past claims. The older questionnaires are being scanned to learn the patterns, and the tool will be aimed at new claims. No veteran's claim or benefit will be reduced or denied because of this effort, according to the department's press secretary.
Alex: For attorneys, the takeaway cuts both ways. If you represent a provider or a claims company, the data is now the first witness.
