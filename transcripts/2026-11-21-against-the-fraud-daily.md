Today, a two million dollar cybersecurity settlement from September is back in court, this time as a shareholder lawsuit, the Justice Department rewrote its own rulebook for False Claims Act cases, and we look at how an insider's network map and a stock chart can prove the same fraud twice.
Alex: Our case of the day starts with a settlement the Justice Department announced on the first of September, and it is back in the news because the fallout is not over.
Jordan: Honeywell Aerospace, the aerospace company that became a standalone public company at the end of June, agreed to pay two million, forty-two thousand, five hundred eighteen dollars to resolve allegations that it violated the False Claims Act by failing to meet cybersecurity requirements in a contract with the Department of Defense.
Alex: The allegations covered April of twenty twenty through December of twenty twenty-three. The government said a Honeywell business unit submitted claims for payment while failing to comply with the cybersecurity controls in National Institute of Standards and Technology Special Publication eight hundred, one seventy-one, on one of its networks, as the contract and the Defense Department's acquisition rules required. The work involved quantum computing services for the Defense Department.
Jordan: The case came from a whistleblower. Rachel Tenney, a former Honeywell information technology employee, filed a qui tam lawsuit in twenty twenty-two. The government intervened in part in August of this year, covering the cybersecurity allegations, and declined the rest. Tenney will receive three hundred seventy-five thousand, eight hundred twenty-three dollars as her share of the settlement, about eighteen percent of the total.
Alex: According to the complaint, Tenney raised the cybersecurity concerns internally and was pushed out of the company. She alleged the company relied on a firewall to protect the network that carried the quantum computing work, a firewall she described as a smokescreen hiding inadequate security practices, with weak malicious code protection, weak access controls, and inadequate testing of incident response.
Jordan: Honeywell did not admit the allegations, and the settlement says the claims are allegations only, with no determination of liability. There was no finding of a hack or a breach. The case was about whether the company met the security standards written into its contract, not whether anyone broke in.
Alex: And here is the second act. This week, a securities class action was filed against Honeywell Aerospace in federal court in Arizona, naming the company and its chief executive, James Currier, and its chief financial officer, Joshua Jepsen, as individual defendants.
Jordan: The complaint covers investors who bought shares between June twenty-ninth and September first of this year, the window from the spin-off to the day the Justice Department announced the settlement. It alleges the company concealed two things: that a small group of suppliers carried a disproportionate share of sales and was facing supply constraints, and that the company was under investigation for potential False Claims Act violations tied to cybersecurity requirements in a Defense Department contract.
Alex: The complaint says investors were told only that False Claims Act enforcement could happen, in general risk language, while a Justice Department inquiry was already underway. And it points to two days the market reacted. On August sixth, after the company reported second quarter results and cut its full year forecast, the stock fell forty-seven dollars and seventeen cents, more than twenty-three percent, to close at a hundred fifty-six dollars and forty-seven cents. On September first, the day the Justice Department announced the settlement, it fell another three dollars and eighty-seven cents, about two and a half percent, to a hundred fifty-four dollars and twenty-four cents.
Jordan: The deadline to apply to lead the case is Monday, November twenty-third. These are allegations, not findings, and the securities case has not been decided. But the pattern matters for this audience: a whistleblower settlement that looked closed on September first is now a second lawsuit, in a second court, with a second set of defendants, the officers themselves.
Alex: The lesson for attorneys is about the tail. A False Claims Act settlement does not end the exposure. It creates a public record, a date, and a dollar figure that securities plaintiffs can build a case around. When you advise a client through a government settlement, advise them about the disclosure question that follows it, because the press release the government issues is the event the market trades on.
Jordan: Now our practice note, and this one is about the Justice Department's own playbook, which changed in September and is only now sinking in.
Alex: On September eighteenth, the department announced two revisions to the Justice Manual, the internal rulebook its lawyers follow in False Claims Act cases. The first limits how the government can use agency guidance. The second changes what happens every time the government declines a whistleblower case.
Jordan: Start with guidance. The revised section, now titled Limitations on Issuance and Use of Guidance Documents, says enforcement actions must rest on violations of applicable legal requirements, not on noncompliance with guidance documents alone. The department may not bring an action based solely on allegations that a company ignored agency guidance.
Alex: That is a real change, and it revives policy from twenty seventeen and twenty eighteen, the Sessions and Brand memoranda, reversing the approach taken under Attorney General Merrick Garland. But note the word solely. Guidance still has uses the manual preserves. It can show what a company knew, it can show industry standards, it can explain technical processes, and, most important for contractors, if a contract requires compliance with agency guidance, it is the contract that makes the guidance matter, not the guidance itself.
Jordan: So a case built only on a Medicare manual or a frequently asked questions page now has a defense argument the manual itself supports. A case built on a contract that incorporates those standards by reference does not. The difference is where the obligation lives, in a statute, a regulation, or a contract, and not in a guidance document standing alone.
Alex: The second revision may matter more for relators. When the government declines to intervene in a qui tam case, its lawyers must now, in every case, assess whether the government should move to dismiss the case outright. The old language said they should also consider dismissal. The new language says they will in each case assess it.
Jordan: The manual also widened the first of the seven dismissal factors, the Granston factors. It used to cover curbing meritless qui tams that facially lack merit. Now it simply says curbing meritless qui tams. The facial limitation is gone, which broadens the set of cases eligible for dismissal. And the department says it may re-evaluate dismissal as the litigation progresses, even after an initial decision to let a case proceed.
Alex: The manual does add a caution. Dismissal will not be warranted in every declined case, because the government often investigates only far enough to decide declination is appropriate, and that is not the same as concluding the case lacks merit. But the direction is clear, and it arrives the same week the Fifth Circuit affirmed the government's dismissal of the Brook Jackson qui tam over Pfizer vaccine trials, applying the Supreme Court's Polansky decision.
Jordan: The practical points, then. For relators and their counsel, a declination letter now comes with a dismissal decision attached, so build the record of merit early, and expect a Granston motion to be part of the conversation from the start. For defense counsel, a theory that begins and ends with guidance is now a live argument, but do not overread it. Contract-incorporated standards survive, and the limitations period for the False Claims Act can reach back ten years, so a record created under one administration's policy can be judged under another's.
Alex: Let us turn to the tech angle, because the Honeywell case was proved twice, once by a network map and once by a stock chart, and both matter.
Jordan: Start with the network. The whistleblower was an information technology employee, and her complaint reads like an audit. The network that carried the quantum computing work for the Defense Department was supposed to meet the hundred and ten security controls in NIST Special Publication eight hundred, one seventy-one, the standard the Defense Department's acquisition rule requires for systems holding covered defense information.
Alex: What she alleged was not a breach but a gap between the paper and the practice. A firewall presented as protection, described in the complaint as a smokescreen. Inadequate malicious code protection. Weak access control. Incident testing and response that was not up to the standard. Oversight of suspicious activity that fell short. That is the pattern in this fall's cyber False Claims Act cases: the government is not waiting for a hack. It is comparing the controls a contract required with the controls that were actually running.
Jordan: Honeywell is the third case in that pattern in just over two months. ILC Dover agreed to pay thirteen million dollars on November third, and Johnson Controls agreed to pay five million dollars on November thirteenth, both over alleged failures to meet the same kind of cybersecurity requirements in federal contracts. Three settlements, three whistleblowers, and in each one the proof was the company's own compliance record.
Alex: And the proof was in the company's own systems. Network diagrams, firewall configurations, control assessments, and the internal complaints the whistleblower raised before she left. For attorneys, that is the discovery list. If you represent a contractor, those documents are the case, and they exist whether or not anyone was ever attacked.
Jordan: Now the second detection layer, the market. The securities complaint is built on disclosure forensics. Two corrective events, two stock drops, and a class period that starts the day the company became standalone and ends the day the government announced the settlement. Plaintiffs' firms now mine Justice Department press releases and securities filings with the same kind of screening the government uses on contractors, looking for the gap between what a company said and what it knew.
Alex: The symmetry is the story. The whistleblower found the gap inside the network. The market found the gap in the disclosures. And both findings came from records the company itself created.
Jordan: One docket note before we close. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission published a notice on November sixteenth asking to extend its whistleblower information collection, the forms for tips and award applications, with public comments due January fifteenth. The Commission estimates about eighteen hundred people a year use those forms, at about half an hour each. It is routine paperwork, but it is also a reminder that the tip pipeline itself is a system the government has to maintain.
Alex: Let us recap today. Honeywell Aerospace paid just over two million dollars to settle cybersecurity False Claims Act allegations brought by a former information technology employee, and now faces a shareholder lawsuit over what it disclosed about that investigation, with a lead plaintiff deadline on Monday. The Justice Department rewrote its manual, limiting guidance-only enforcement and requiring a dismissal assessment in every declined qui tam case. And the proof in these cases keeps coming from the same place, the records a company creates, in its networks and in its filings.
