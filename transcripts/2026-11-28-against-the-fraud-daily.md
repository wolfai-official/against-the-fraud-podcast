## Transcript

Alex: Welcome back to Against the Fraud Daily. It is Saturday, November twenty-eighth, twenty twenty-six, and we are closing out a short Thanksgiving week with a jury verdict that should worry anyone who runs a clinical trial, and a new Justice Department task force that puts artificial intelligence at the center of fraud enforcement. I am Alex.
Jordan: And I am Jordan. Today we have a Florida doctor and three of his staff convicted of faking asthma drug trial data, a Justice Department task force with a new name for artificial intelligence and a deadline that lands today, and the detection lesson that ties the two stories together.
Alex: Let us start in South Florida. On Monday, November twenty-third, the Justice Department announced that a federal jury in the Southern District of Florida convicted a medical doctor and three staff members of Pines Care Research Center, a medical research center in Pembroke Pines, for falsifying data in clinical trials of prospective new drug treatments. A fifth staff member had already pleaded guilty.
Jordan: Who was convicted, and what exactly did the jury find they did?
Alex: The doctor is Jaynier Moya, fifty years old, from Southwest Ranches. He co-owned Pines Care and served as the principal investigator for its studies. The three staff members are clinical research coordinators: Luis Montano, fifty-six, of Hialeah; Yuniarka Garcia, forty-one, of Plantation; and Luis Cuza, forty-two, of Cape Coral. The jury convicted Moya, Montano, and Garcia of wire fraud and conspiracy to commit wire fraud. Cuza was convicted of conspiracy to commit wire fraud. The fifth employee, Alexandra Olivera, thirty-eight, also of Hialeah, pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit wire fraud back in August.
Jordan: And the scheme itself. What were they doing?
Alex: Pines Care had been selected as a trial site to gather human testing data on the safety and effectiveness of prospective new asthma treatments, on behalf of a pharmaceutical company sponsor. The Justice Department does not name the sponsor or the drugs. Beginning no later than twenty nineteen, according to court documents and evidence presented at trial, the defendants fabricated testing data and falsified records while conducting those trials.
Jordan: Fabricated how? Walk through the mechanics, because this is the part that matters for anyone who reviews trial data.
Alex: There were three steps. First, they used identification documents belonging to people who never actually took part in the studies, and built records that made it appear those people were participating as research subjects. Second, they falsified records to make it look as if those people had taken the study medication and completed the testing the study protocols required. Third, they paid other individuals to undergo the tests, and attributed that data to the purported research subjects. Then they submitted the fabricated test data to the sponsor to obtain payment for research that had not actually been carried out.
Jordan: So the sponsor was paying for completed research, and getting data attached to people who were never in the study.
Alex: That is what the trial evidence showed, and the jury believed it. The Justice Department's announcement quotes Assistant Attorney General A. Tysen Duva, of the Criminal Division, saying accurate human testing data is critical to the Food and Drug Administration's evaluation of whether to approve new drug treatments for Americans, and that the defendants abused their positions to falsify human testing data gathered to evaluate the safety and efficacy of new asthma medicine.
Jordan: What happens next for the defendants?
Alex: Sentencing is set for February ninth, twenty twenty-seven, for Moya, Montano, Garcia, and Cuza. Olivera is scheduled to be sentenced on December fifteenth, twenty twenty-six. Moya, Montano, and Garcia each face up to eighty years in prison. Cuza faces up to twenty years. A federal judge will determine the actual sentences, taking into account the sentencing guidelines and other statutory factors. Those maximums are statutory ceilings, not predictions. The investigation was led by the Food and Drug Administration's Office of Criminal Investigations, Miami Field Office.
Jordan: This was not the first time this case was in the news. The charges were announced months ago.
Alex: Right. The case first became public on June tenth, twenty twenty-six, when the Justice Department announced an indictment against Moya, Montano, and Garcia, and a separate criminal information against Olivera. At that point the charges were only allegations, and the defendants were presumed innocent. This week's verdict is the first outcome at trial, and it changes the posture completely. These are now convictions, decided by a jury on the evidence.
Jordan: And there is a pattern here worth naming. Local reporting notes that this verdict is the latest in a run of federal prosecutions over faked drug trial data at small research sites in South Florida. That matters well beyond Broward County, because regulators rely on that data to decide which medicines reach pharmacy shelves.
Alex: Which brings us to the practice note, because this week also brought a structural change at the Justice Department that every fraud lawyer should understand. On November fifth, the department announced the formation of a Super Intelligence Task Force.
Jordan: Super Intelligence, not artificial intelligence. That is a deliberate rebrand.
Alex: It is. In September, the president signed an executive order titled Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence, directing the executive branch to use the term Super Intelligence in place of artificial intelligence to the maximum extent permitted by law. On October thirteenth, an internal Justice Department memorandum established the task force, and the public announcement followed on November fifth. It is led by Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward and Associate Deputy Attorney General Stephen Weiss.
Jordan: What does the task force actually do?
Alex: It centralizes the department's approach to enforcement involving advanced artificial intelligence. It will organize criminal and civil enforcement efforts involving the technology, deconflict investigations and cases among Justice Department components and United States Attorneys' Offices, recommend enforcement priorities, serve as the department's point of contact with the White House task force and other federal agencies, and develop guidance on prosecuting unlawful conduct involving the technology and on evaluating compliance measures. It also absorbs the work of the Artificial Intelligence Litigation Task Force, which was set up in January to challenge certain state artificial intelligence laws.
Jordan: And the stated mission is about misuse.
Alex: Yes. The department says the task force will ensure that American leadership in this technology is not compromised by its misuse by foreign state and state-sponsored actors, transnational criminal organizations, and individual or corporate actors engaged in fraud or other unlawful conduct. It is also charged with taking affirmative measures to promote American dominance in the field.
Jordan: Here is the part attorneys need to hear clearly. Does this create a new offense, a new law companies can violate?
Alex: No, and that is the key point. Neither the announcement nor the memorandum names a new offense or a specific statute. The attorney general has said he will not investigate or prosecute artificial intelligence companies when no statute is being violated. So for now, enforcement would run through existing laws. That is exactly where the False Claims Act comes in.
Jordan: Connect that for us. Where does a task force about artificial intelligence meet a qui tam case?
Alex: In three places. First, a company's use of artificial intelligence in connection with government payment claims can itself be a false claim, if the company certifies results it did not actually produce, or bills for work a system did not do. Second, cybersecurity certifications and representations made while negotiating and performing government contracts are already the subject of False Claims Act settlements this fall. If a company leans on an artificial intelligence tool to make those representations, the same exposure applies. And third, the task force is developing guidance on how prosecutors should evaluate compliance measures, which means the compliance programs your clients build around artificial intelligence use will eventually be judged against a published standard.
Jordan: There is also a notification process in the memorandum, right?
Alex: Yes. Justice Department components and United States Attorneys' Offices are supposed to notify the task force when they seek to open an investigation, and before seeking charges or otherwise finalizing a resolution in matters involving the technology. The memorandum says that notification should not delay action in exigent circumstances, including imminent threats to public safety or risk of loss of evidence. Practically, that means artificial intelligence cases will now be visible to department leadership earlier, and resolutions will be coordinated across offices. If you are negotiating a resolution that touches on this technology, expect more eyes on it.
Jordan: And there is a deadline today.
Alex: There is. The September executive order gave the White House science adviser sixty days to submit proposed legislative language for a federal definition of the term, and that sixty-day clock runs out today, November twenty-eighth. So watch for a definition proposal in the coming days, because the definition will shape what counts as the technology the task force polices.
Jordan: Let us turn to the tech angle, because the Pines Care case is really a story about data, and about how fabricated data gets caught.
Alex: It is. Think about what the defendants had to fake. A clinical trial is a chain of records. Enrollment records say who the subject is. Visit records say the subject showed up. Dosing records say the subject took the medication. Lab results say what happened next. Payment records say the sponsor paid for completed work. Every link in that chain has to match the others, and the Pines Care scheme broke the chain at the first link, because the named subjects were not the people who were tested.
Jordan: The stand-ins are the tell.
Alex: Exactly. Paying real people to sit for tests generates real test results, but those results belong to the wrong bodies. The moment an investigator compares the identity documents to the people who actually walked into the clinic, or compares the test results to the medical histories of the named subjects, the mismatch appears. That is why the Food and Drug Administration's criminal investigators were able to build this case. They did not need to second-guess the science. They needed to follow the paper, and the paper did not add up.
Jordan: This is the same theme we saw earlier this week, in a very different posture.
Alex: Yes. On Monday's episode we covered the Fifth Circuit's decision in the Ventavia case, where the government moved to dismiss a relator's suit challenging the integrity of Pfizer's Covid vaccine trials. There, the dispute was about whether the government could end a private suit. Here, the government brought its own criminal case and a jury convicted. Two different doors into the same building. Clinical trial data is government-facing data, and when it is fabricated, the exposure runs in both directions, civil and criminal.
Jordan: What should sponsors and trial sites take from this?
Alex: Three things. First, identity verification at enrollment is not paperwork, it is the control that makes every later record trustworthy. Second, source document verification, matching the test result to the person who actually produced it, is the audit step that catches this kind of fraud. And third, the payment trail is evidence. The defendants submitted fabricated data to get paid, which means the sponsor's own payment records became part of the proof. If you run a trial site, your records are your defense, and if you run a sponsor, your records are your early warning system.
Jordan: And zooming out, the two stories today point in the same direction.
Alex: They do. The Pines Care verdict shows the government prosecuting fabricated data the old-fashioned way, with investigators following records. The Super Intelligence Task Force shows the government organizing itself for a world in which the data, and the fraud, are increasingly produced by machines. Attorneys on both sides should be ready for both. The record-keeping cases are not going away, and the artificial intelligence cases are just getting organized.
Jordan: A few dates to keep on the calendar before we close. The Justice Department's response in the Kalshi prediction market matter is due December eleventh. The Securities and Exchange Commission's response in the Michael Bacon whistleblower award case is due December twenty-first. And Alexandra Olivera, the Pines Care coordinator who pleaded guilty, is scheduled to be sentenced on December fifteenth.
Alex: Let us recap. A federal jury in South Florida convicted a doctor and three staff members of Pines Care Research Center of falsifying asthma drug trial data, using identification documents from people who never participated and paying stand-ins to generate the results. The Justice Department launched a Super Intelligence Task Force to coordinate criminal and civil enforcement involving artificial intelligence, with no new offense created, but with new visibility and guidance coming. And the detection lesson is the same in both stories. Fraud leaves a paper trail, and the people who follow it carefully are the ones who catch it.
Jordan: That is Against the Fraud Daily for Saturday, November twenty-eighth, twenty twenty-six. I am Jordan.
Alex: And I am Alex. We will be back tomorrow with the newest whistleblower and government fraud developments. Until then, keep your records straight, and keep watching the docket.
