Alex: Let's start with the case of the day, and honestly, it's two cases, because the Justice Department dropped them both on Friday, November sixth, and they tell the same story from two different angles.
Jordan: First, Lab Genomics, a clinical laboratory in Fountain Valley, California, and its owner, Leena Dalal. They agreed to pay one million two hundred fifty thousand dollars to resolve allegations that they violated the False Claims Act by paying illegal kickbacks to marketers.
Alex: The allegation is straightforward. From January of twenty twenty through September of twenty twenty-two, Lab Genomics and Dalal allegedly paid commissions based on the value and volume of referrals to three independent contractor marketers, to get health care providers to order and refer lab tests to the company.
Jordan: And here's the part that matters. The government alleges the lab knew those payments did not comply with the Anti-Kickback Statute, and structured invoices to the marketers to make the commission payments look like flat fees. The paperwork said one thing. The money said another.
Alex: The settlement resolves part of a whistleblower lawsuit filed by Sunil Wadhwa and Ken Newton. They will receive two hundred twenty-five thousand dollars of the settlement, about eighteen percent. The government had already resolved similar allegations in the same case against two other companies, Admera Health and Next Bio-Research Services.

Alex: Now the second lab case from the same day, and it's the bigger one. Quest Diagnostics, based in Secaucus, New Jersey, paid just over thirteen million dollars, plus interest, to resolve allegations that it submitted false claims to Medicare and to the New York and New Jersey Medicaid programs.
Jordan: The allegation here is not cash commissions. It's football. From July of twenty thirteen through August of twenty twenty-two, Quest allegedly used its sponsorship deal with the New York Giants to offer doctors and health care executives free suite seats at MetLife Stadium, food and alcohol, VIP visits to training camp, draft chalk talks with former players, and family combine days, all to induce referrals of lab business.
Alex: Three whistleblowers brought the case, Doctor Gerard Abate, Lori Anderson, and Doctor Douglas Rabin. They will share just under two and a half million dollars of the recovery. Quest denies the allegations and says it agreed to the settlement last year to put the matter behind it. There is no determination of liability, and the settlement does not rule out a future criminal probe.
Jordan: Quest also signed a five-year corporate integrity agreement with the health and human services inspector general. That is the part that outlives the press release. Five years of federal compliance oversight, and every future dealing with a referral source under a microscope.
Alex: Put the two cases together and the pattern is hard to miss. Laboratories live or die on referral volume, and the Anti-Kickback Statute draws a hard line. Paying for referrals, in cash or in suite seats, turns the resulting Medicare claims false. Two labs, two settlements, one Friday.
Jordan: That brings us to the practice note, and this one is about the survival of the qui tam mechanism itself. On September first, the Eleventh Circuit decided United States ex rel. Zafirov against Florida Medical Associates, and it reversed a district court ruling that had declared the False Claims Act's qui tam provisions unconstitutional.
Alex: Some background. In September of twenty twenty-four, Judge Kathryn Kimball Mizelle in the Middle District of Florida became the first judge to hold that qui tam relators violate the Appointments Clause, reasoning that private citizens were exercising significant government authority without being appointed as officers of the United States. She dismissed the case entirely.
Jordan: The Eleventh Circuit disagreed. It held that relators do not hold a continuing position, and that is enough to decide they are not officers who must be appointed by the President. The decision joins the Fifth, Sixth, Ninth, and Tenth Circuits, every other circuit to consider the question, in rejecting the Appointments Clause challenge.
Alex: But here is the part attorneys need to hear clearly. The court decided only the Appointments Clause issue. It expressly declined to reach the defendants' other two constitutional arguments, under the Take Care Clause and the Vesting Clause, and sent those back to the district court to decide first.
Jordan: So the practical takeaway is threefold. First, if you are defending a qui tam case, an Appointments Clause dismissal motion has now lost in five circuits, and it is a weak reed to lean on. Second, if you represent a relator, expect the motion to keep being filed anyway, because the remaining challenges give defendants a second and third bite. And third, watch the remand. If the district court dismisses on Take Care or Vesting grounds, the case comes right back to the Eleventh Circuit, and the Supreme Court may yet weigh in.
Alex: The bottom line for practice: qui tam is alive, the constitutional attack is narrowing, but it is not over. Do not close the compliance manual yet, as one commentary put it, and do not build a litigation strategy on a constitutional argument five circuits have now rejected.
Jordan: Now the tech angle, and it connects directly to both of today's cases. Neither the Lab Genomics case nor the Quest case turned on a whistleblower sneaking documents out of a building. They turned on data, on lining up two sets of records and watching them disagree.
Alex: Take Lab Genomics. The invoices said flat fee. But the payments moved with the value and volume of referrals. You do not need a confession to prove that. You need the payment records on one side, the referral and claims records on the other, and the math in between. When a so-called flat fee rises and falls with the number of tests ordered, the flat fee is a fiction.
Jordan: Quest is the same lesson at a bigger scale. Nine years of entertainment, suite seats, training camp visits, and draft events, against nine years of referral patterns from the same doctors and executives. The government did not have to prove what anyone was thinking. It had to show the pattern, who got the perks and whose referrals followed.
Alex: For attorneys, the lesson is about discovery. Subpoena the payment data early, and subpoena the referral data early, because the gap between the two is often the whole case. For compliance officers, the lesson runs the other way. If your marketing invoices say flat fee but the math says commission, assume the government will find the gap. Claims data, payment records, and referral logs are all machine-readable now, and the agencies that enforce the False Claims Act compare them routinely.
Jordan: And that is the thread that runs through this whole show. The fraud in these cases was not hidden in a vault. It was hidden in plain sight, in records the companies themselves created. The technology did not invent the case. It just made the contradiction impossible to ignore.
Alex: One quick note before we close. The securities whistleblower program is rebounding. This year looks different. April brought an award of more than fifty million dollars, June brought awards of about eight hundred thousand, seven million, and twenty million dollars, and a September order from the commission itself awarded more than twelve million dollars to one claimant. If you have a securities client sitting on the fence, the program is paying again.
