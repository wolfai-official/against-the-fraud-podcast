On Monday, the Justice Department announced that three companies that held federal contracts through the Department of Energy have agreed to settle allegations that they deceptively obtained forgiveness of their Paycheck Protection Program loans, in violation of the False Claims Act.

Congress created the Paycheck Protection Program in March of twenty twenty, to help small businesses keep workers on the payroll during the pandemic. A business could borrow money, and the Small Business Administration would forgive the loan, so long as the money went to payroll and other approved expenses. But the borrower had to certify that it actually incurred those payroll costs.
Jordan: The Justice Department says these three companies certified payroll costs on their forgiveness applications, and the Small Business Administration approved the forgiveness. The problem, according to the government, is that the companies had already billed those same payroll costs to the Department of Energy under existing federal contracts, and the department had already paid them. In other words, the taxpayers paid for the same payroll twice. Once through the contract, and once through loan forgiveness.
Alex: The investigation was run by the Department of Energy's inspector general, out of its Knoxville field office. And the inspector general's office put it more bluntly. Companies that were already being reimbursed for payroll under federal contracts should not have claimed those same expenses on forgiveness applications.
Alex: The legal theory here is simple, and it is why the False Claims Act fits so neatly. A forgiveness application is a claim on the government. Certify costs you did not actually bear, and the certification itself is false. That is the false claim. And the exposure is not just the money back. The act allows treble damages plus penalties on every false claim, which is why even a one million dollar settlement can start from a much smaller loan.

Alex: And that brings us to our practice note, because the courts just gave qui tam relators another reason to keep filing. Last Thursday, November twelfth, the Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals reversed a Florida trial court in a case called Brent Berry against Native American Services Corporation.
Jordan: The trial court had thrown the case out on the pleadings, ruling that the False Claims Act's qui tam provisions violate the Appointments Clause of the Constitution. That is the argument that a private citizen who sues on behalf of the United States is exercising government power without being properly appointed as an officer. The Eleventh Circuit said no. It granted the relators' and the government's motion for summary reversal, in one sentence that matters. Because the court's earlier decision in Zafirov controls the outcome of this appeal.
Alex: Zafirov is the September first decision we covered last week, where the Eleventh Circuit held that qui tam relators are not officers under the Appointments Clause, because they hold no continuing position and exercise no ongoing government authority. Five federal appeals courts have now rejected these constitutional challenges. The Fifth, Sixth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh circuits.
Jordan: The Berry case is not over. The Eleventh Circuit sent it back to the trial court, which still has to consider the defendants' other constitutional arguments, under the Take Care Clause and the Vesting Clause. The Appointments Clause attack on qui tam is now a dead end in the Eleventh Circuit, and a court there will reverse it summarily, without full briefing. If you are defending a qui tam case, do not build your strategy on that argument. And if you are a relator, a dismissal on that ground is now highly vulnerable on appeal.
Alex: One more procedural point worth noting. A summary reversal is the court deciding the appeal on the papers, on a motion, because the answer is already settled by binding precedent. That is how fast Zafirov is now working in practice. Two months after the decision, it is disposing of copycat dismissals without a full round of briefing. For relators, that means less time and money spent defending the existence of qui tam itself, and more spent on the merits of the fraud.

First, screening. Every application is compared automatically against public and private databases, checking for inconsistencies. Second, data analytics. Tools, sometimes using machine learning, flag files with data anomalies that need a closer look. Third, human review. People examine the flagged files. And fourth, referral to the inspector general when a file looks likely fraudulent.
Alex: And the machinery is getting bigger. In July, the Small Business Administration announced it is expanding its use of Palantir software, using advanced technology and artificial intelligence to surface leads, support criminal enforcement, and recover taxpayer money. Jordan: Meanwhile, the Justice Department is courting the other side of the data equation. The whistleblowers who find fraud in public data. It invites data miners to come in and explain how their tools and signals reliably point to fraud. The department says data miners have filed nearly half of all qui tam complaints since fiscal year twenty twenty-four, and that more than seven hundred eighty qui tam complaints have already been filed in fiscal year twenty twenty-six.
Alex: Put it together, and the lesson for attorneys is straightforward. The government is matching loan forgiveness applications against contract payment records, tax filings, and payroll data. In fiscal year twenty twenty-five alone, the Justice Department announced more than two hundred pandemic-related settlements and judgments, worth more than two hundred thirty million dollars.
