Alex: Let us start with the case of the day. Premier Medical Group, a multi-physician practice based in Clarksville, Tennessee, has agreed to pay two point eight million dollars to resolve allegations that it violated the False Claims Act by improperly billing Medicare and TRICARE.
Jordan: The United States Attorney for the Middle District of Tennessee announced the settlement on November second. The government alleged that from November first, twenty eighteen, through May thirty-first, twenty twenty-three, Premier submitted claims to Medicare and TRICARE for services performed by non-physician practitioners, nurse practitioners and physician assistants, using physicians' National Provider Identifier numbers.
Alex: That is the heart of it. The services were performed by one group of clinicians, and the claims went out under a different clinician's billing number. The practice did not admit liability, and the government says the claims resolved by the settlement are allegations only, with no determination of liability. There is also no corporate integrity agreement attached to this one.
Jordan: Medicare and TRICARE are the two federal programs here, which matters for the story, because TRICARE covers service members, military retirees, and their families. The investigation was run by the United States Attorney's Office for the Middle District of Tennessee, with assistance from the Defense Criminal Investigative Service, the criminal investigative arm of the Defense Department's inspector general.
Alex: Now, why would a billing number matter so much? This is where the tech angle comes in, and it is really the key to understanding the case. Under Medicare's rules, when a nurse practitioner or a physician assistant bills under their own number, Medicare pays eighty-five percent of the physician fee schedule amount for the same service.
Jordan: But there is a separate rule, called incident-to billing, where a service provided by auxiliary staff can be billed under a supervising physician's number, and Medicare then pays one hundred percent. That is only allowed when strict conditions are met. The physician has to have started the care, stayed involved, and provided the required supervision.
Alex: So the gap between eighty-five percent and one hundred percent is the financial incentive, and the billing number on the claim is the switch that controls which rate gets paid. And that switch leaves a data trail, which is how cases like this one get built.
Jordan: Exactly. Think about what an investigator can check without ever setting foot in the clinic. First, volume. A single physician's billing number can only account for so many patient visits in a day. When claims data shows one doctor's number attached to more visits than one person could possibly see, that is a red flag.
Alex: Second, cross-matching. The claim says the physician provided the service, but the electronic health record has an audit log showing which clinician actually opened the chart, wrote the note, and signed it. The appointment schedule shows who was in the exam room. When the billing number and the clinical record point to two different people, over and over, across four and a half years, that pattern is hard to explain away.
Jordan: And third, the payment data itself. Investigators can compare what was paid against what should have been paid if the services had been billed under the correct practitioner's number. The difference between the two rates, multiplied across thousands of claims, is how the government sizes the loss. No whistleblower testimony is needed to establish the arithmetic. The data does the talking.
Alex: A note for the attorneys listening. This settlement was announced as the result of a government investigation, not as a qui tam case, and the release does not name a relator. That is worth flagging, because it shows the government is finding these billing patterns through its own data work, not only through whistleblowers walking in the door.
Jordan: Which brings us to the practice note, and this one is big. On October twenty-second, the Justice Department's Civil Division and the Department of Health and Human Services announced a new six-month pilot program: the Healthcare False Claims Act Self-Disclosure Pilot Program.
Alex: Here is how it works. A health care company that discovers a potential False Claims Act violation, one the government does not already know about, can come forward voluntarily and get a coordinated resolution of its civil liability from the Justice Department and Health and Human Services together.
Jordan: The terms are strikingly favorable compared to a normal settlement. The company pays a repayment of one point two five to one point six times its base repayment amount, meaning single damages, not treble damages. There are no factual admissions required. There is no corporate integrity agreement. There are no civil monetary penalties from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, and no enrollment revocation.
Alex: The exact multiplier within that range turns on factors like how quickly the company came forward, how long the conduct went on, whether senior leadership was involved, and how promptly the company fixed the problem. And the window is short. Complete disclosures have to be in by April twenty-second, twenty twenty-seven. Late or partial submissions will not be reviewed.
Jordan: A few limits to keep in mind. The pilot covers civil violations only. If the conduct might be criminal, it goes through a separate channel, a coordination policy for criminal health care disclosures that the Justice Department's National Fraud Enforcement Division and the inspector general announced on October fifteenth. And if the government already knows about the conduct, the company cannot use the pilot, though it can still seek cooperation credit under the Justice Manual.
Alex: Here is why this matters right now for anyone advising a health care client. If your client has a billing problem sitting in a drawer, there is now a published, predictable price for bringing it forward, and a hard deadline. That changes the advice. The question is no longer whether to disclose in the abstract. It is whether to disclose before April twenty-second, and whether the conduct is still unknown to the government, because once a whistleblower files or an investigation starts, the pilot door closes.
Jordan: One more piece of context, and it is a watch item. The pilot was announced by the Civil Division, the division that runs False Claims Act enforcement. On Monday, November ninth, the head of that division, Assistant Attorney General Brett Shumate, resigned, effective immediately. He was the fourth senior Justice Department official to step down since the midterm elections.
Alex: We do not know why he left, and we should not speculate. But for practitioners, the practical question is continuity. The pilot program was launched under his division, and it runs through next April. Attorneys will be watching whether the new leadership keeps the program on the same terms, and whether the pace of False Claims Act cases changes while the division is in transition.
Jordan: Before we close, one quick docket note. On November sixth, a federal judge in the Northern District of New York unsealed a qui tam case called Worpole against Hudson Valley Hospice. Declined cases are often where the next big relator-share fight happens, so keep an eye on it.

Premier Medical Group in Clarksville, Tennessee, pays two point eight million dollars over allegations that services by nurse practitioners and physician assistants were billed under physicians' numbers.
