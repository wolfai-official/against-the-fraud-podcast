## Case of the day

**Clarksville Pain Institute / Pain Institute of Nashville — $1.5M medically-unnecessary-testing settlement (announced Nov 12, 2026).**
Clarksville Pain Institute, LLC; Pain Institute of Nashville, PLC; and managers Michael Cox and Debbie Cox agreed to pay $1.5 million to resolve False Claims Act allegations that, from April 2014 through May 2024, they submitted or caused the submission of false claims to Medicare and the Department of Veterans Affairs for medically unnecessary diagnostic testing — urine drug testing, allergy testing, and psychological testing. The government's complaint alleged the clinics pressured staff to bill for more testing than was medically necessary, routinely tested patients' urine without reviewing the results, and in some cases required patients to agree to unnecessary testing to receive their pain medications. The claims are allegations only with no determination of liability. The qui tam case, *United States ex rel. Nicholson v. Clarksville Pain Institute, et al.*, No. 3:20-cv-00309 (M.D. Tenn.), was filed under seal in April 2020 by former employee Krista Nicholson; the United States intervened in part in July 2024 and filed a complaint-in-intervention on September 11, 2024. Nicholson receives $285,000 (19% of the recovery). The case nearly died on pleading: the court dismissed the government's complaint without prejudice for failing Rule 9(b)'s heightened pleading standard for fraud as to the Cox defendants, then granted the government's motion to amend on April 2, 2026, after it added detail from former employees and a patient. Investigated by the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Middle District of Tennessee, HHS-OIG, and VA-OIG. Same city as Premier Medical Group (covered Nov 11) but a different clinic and a different scheme — Premier was NPI billing; this is unnecessary testing.

## FCA practice note

**The organizational relator: Deloitte's $21.5M Civil Rights Fraud Initiative settlement (announced Aug 25, 2026).** Deloitte LLP and four affiliated entities agreed to pay $21.5 million to resolve allegations that, from 2017 to the present, they falsely certified compliance with anti-discrimination requirements in their federal contracts while engaging in race- and sex-based employment practices and charging the costs of those practices to the government. It was the second settlement under DOJ's Civil Rights Fraud Initiative (launched May 2025), after IBM's $17 million in April 2026; Accenture and Lockheed Martin ($29.5 million, covered Nov 9) followed as the third and fourth. The relator was not an employee — it was the American Alliance for Equal Rights, a nonprofit, with the information coming from one of its members, a former senior Deloitte employee identified only as "Member A." The Alliance received $4,300,000 (20% of the federal recovery), a comparatively high share for a case settled before real litigation. The Alliance also brought state-law claims, and Deloitte settled separately with Florida and Indiana for $1.2 million each. Commentators noted Deloitte appears to have received cooperation credit under DOJ's guidelines for disclosure, cooperation, and remediation. As part of the deal, the Alliance agreed to confidentially disclose the source employee's identity to Deloitte. Takeaways: a relator need not be on the payroll — an organization with a member-insider can carry a qui tam; state-law exposure can multiply a single set of facts into multiple settlements; and cooperation credit is a real lever in settlement talks.

## Tech angle

Medically unnecessary testing is proved through claims-data pattern analysis: the same battery of tests billed patient after patient regardless of condition, urine screens ordered with results never reviewed, tests that never change treatment. Investigators compare what was billed against what the patient's chart shows — the gap is the evidence — and the human record (former employees and a patient, added in the government's amended complaint) supplies the pressure behind the pattern. The same principle as the image forensics in the Dana-Farber case: the fraud leaves a file, and the file does not forget.

**AI-whistleblower watch:** Today, Nov 13, is the deadline for OpenAI's answers to the Oct 29 letter from 20 House Democrats led by Rep. Greg Casar, which asked 11 sets of questions about the firing of three safety researchers and a list of every security incident under investigation; as of this morning there is no public sign of an answer. The researchers themselves have moved: the *Wall Street Journal* reports Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak, and Mikita Balesni have written directly to OpenAI's board and safety committees, urging the company to preserve its ability to monitor models' chain-of-thought reasoning and to work with independent safety auditors. OpenAI says it fired the three for mishandling sensitive information, not for raising safety concerns; the researchers dispute that and say they never shared confidential information outside the company. On Oct 30, OpenAI published a new Raising Concerns Policy covering anonymous reporting without retaliation. Separately, three senior House Democrats wrote to five frontier AI companies in late October asking for a full accounting of model misalignment and safety incidents by Nov 11 and notice within 48 hours of any new incident — incident reporting on a clock, not at a company's convenience.
