## Case of the day

**SEC fiscal 2026 enforcement results (announced Nov 10, 2026).** The Securities and Exchange Commission filed 348 enforcement actions in the fiscal year ended September 30, 2026 — 235 standalone actions and 113 follow-on administrative proceedings — down about 24% from 456 the year before, and obtained orders for about $1.095 billion in monetary relief ($752 million in disgorgement and prejudgment interest, $343 million in civil penalties). The more telling number: the Commission returned about $731 million to more than 250,000 harmed investors, nearly triple the $262 million returned in fiscal 2025. The whistleblower program received about 26,000 tips and awarded more than $158 million to 65 individual whistleblowers, the highest number of individual awardees since fiscal 2023 — a sharp rebound from fiscal 2025 ($60 million to 48 people) and below fiscal 2024 ($255 million). Enforcement Director David Woodcock, speaking the same evening at the 26th Annual A.A. Sommer Lecture at Fordham Law School, said "activity and effectiveness are not the same" and that case counts cannot show whether fraud was stopped early or money reached victims. Forty-five of the 235 standalone actions targeted firms the SEC says filed false Forms ADV to pose as investment advisers, in two waves: six entities in November 2025 and 39 in August 2026. The release also notes the first significant update to the Enforcement Manual since 2017 and the rescission of Rule 202.5(e), the "no-admit, no-deny" policy. Tie-in: Michael Bacon's Third Circuit fight over his SEC award cut (covered Nov 15) continues — the SEC's response is due December 21, 2026 — so the program can be paying more whistleblowers while one whistleblower argues the Commission broke the law by shrinking his award below the Dodd-Frank 10% floor.

## FCA practice note

**The CFTC's new 30% presumption for smaller whistleblower awards — and the causation bar that still applies.** On September 11, 2026, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission approved a final rule (published September 16, effective October 16, 2026) creating a presumption that a whistleblower whose tip leads to collected sanctions of up to about $16.66 million will receive 30% of the money collected — the statutory maximum — where the resulting award is $5 million or less. The rule is modeled on SEC Rule 21F-6(c). The CFTC estimates about 82% of meritorious awards will fall under the presumption, which is meant to cut the average two-and-a-half-year wait from claim to final award. The presumption does not apply if the whistleblower was culpable in the underlying violations, unreasonably delayed reporting, or interfered with the entity's internal compliance and reporting systems. Context: since its first award in 2014, the CFTC has paid more than $580 million to whistleblowers tied to more than $5.1 billion in monetary sanctions; three days after approving the rule, it announced 10 awards totaling more than $150 million. Caution: *The Estate of Jennions v. CFTC*, No. 25-1106 (D.C. Cir. July 28, 2026) — a former bank employee gave information to Britain's Financial Conduct Authority about alleged manipulation of foreign exchange benchmark rates, the CFTC later brought actions against five banks, but the D.C. Circuit upheld the denial of his award because the investigation was prompted by a detailed media article, not his information. Takeaways: (1) file early, before the story is public; (2) document exactly what original information the client provided and how it connects to the enforcement action, because an award can still be denied if the agency traces its investigation to another source.

## Tech angle

**The SEC's new Office of Super Intelligence and Analytics — AI moves from enforcement subject to enforcement tool.** The Division of Enforcement created the Office of Super Intelligence and Analytics to bring advanced analytics into investigative workflows and "force-multiply" resources: AI-enabled anomaly detection, accelerated document and transcript review, and improved blockchain and financial-flow analysis. Director Woodcock stressed the point is not to replace human judgment but to give staff better information sooner. The SEC already used data analytics for years — the ARTEMIS system cross-references trading data against corporate events in insider trading investigations, plus risk-based tools to flag potential earnings management. The Retail Fraud Working Group now uses AI and data from multiple regulatory and law enforcement sources to generate investigative leads, and the SEC reports more than a dozen new investigations have already resulted. The 45 fake-adviser cases came from screening registration data, not a whistleblower tip. Practical warning: with generative tools reviewing document productions and spotting themes traditional text analytics miss, subpoenas and document requests will arrive faster, more targeted, and with shorter deadlines — and the Commission is still policing overstated AI claims by public companies, so the technology is both a tool and a subject of enforcement.

**Docket note:** On November 10, 2026, the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Southern District of Illinois announced that Interstate Group Holdings, Inc., a Virginia moving company, agreed to pay $325,000 to resolve False Claims Act allegations that it gave service members improper service recovery payments, inconvenience payments, and modest gifts — some for the purpose of influencing Customer Satisfaction Survey (CSS) scores — between August 2017 and February 2023. The company moved household goods for service members under the Defense Personal Property Program (DP3), administered by the U.S. Transportation Command at Scott Air Force Base; CSS scores partly decide how many shipments a company is awarded, so a better score means more government-paid business. Pattern: about a year earlier, Denali Group agreed to pay $3.5 million after the government alleged its employees submitted false CSS surveys while posing as service members in the same program. The investigation was conducted by the Department of War Office of Inspector General, Air Force Office of Special Investigations, Army Criminal Investigation Division, and Defense Contract Audit Agency. The claims are allegations only; there has been no determination of liability.
