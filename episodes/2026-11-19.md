## Case of the day

**Anthony "Andy" Sanborn / Win Win Win LLC d/b/a Concord Casino pays $510,454.44 to resolve potential False Claims Act civil claims over misuse of pandemic relief funds (announced Nov 17, 2026, D.N.H.).** Sanborn, a former New Hampshire state senator and owner of the Concord Casino, received approximately $844,000 through the Small Business Administration's Economic Injury Disaster Loan (EIDL) program for his company, Win Win Win, LLC. EIDL loans were expanded under the CARES Act to give small businesses low-interest working capital, and borrowers had to certify the money would be used only for authorized business purposes. The United States found that Sanborn misused more than $255,000 of the loan proceeds for personal expenditures unrelated to the casino, including the purchase of a 2006 Porsche Cayman in January 2022 for $48,750. Sanborn has now repaid twice the value of the funds he misdirected: the total settlement of $510,454.44 includes $255,232.72 he already paid as restitution in his federal criminal proceedings. The civil claims are potential claims only — allegations, with no determination of liability. The criminal side is separate: Sanborn pleaded guilty in July 2026 to one count of theft of government funds, is scheduled to be sentenced in January, faces up to 10 years in prison, and prosecutors have said they will recommend a year and a day. The matter was investigated by the FBI, IRS Criminal Investigations, and the U.S. Postal Inspection Service. In 2023, New Hampshire gaming regulators moved to revoke Sanborn's gaming license and force the sale of Concord Casino over his handling of the pandemic aid. Context: pandemic fraud enforcement is still producing cases years after the money went out — in fiscal 2025 alone, the government reached more than 200 pandemic-related False Claims Act settlements and judgments worth more than $230 million. The lesson for attorneys: the loan certification is the claim, and the paper trail — a certification on one side, a car purchase on the other — is the case.

## FCA practice note

**The SEC's whistleblower report puts a price on waiting: 29 awards were cut for unreasonable delay.** The SEC's Office of the Whistleblower published its Annual Report to Congress for fiscal 2026 on November 16, 2026. The program granted 65 individual whistleblower awards totaling more than $158 million — the most people awarded since fiscal 2023 — and issued final orders denying awards to 140 individuals, with 50 preliminary determinations recommending awards. The number that should change how attorneys counsel clients: in fiscal 2026, the Commission reduced 29 awards because the whistleblower unreasonably delayed reporting, and 6 more for culpability. The Commission's stated reason is that timely reporting lets it act quickly to protect investors, gather evidence before it becomes harder to reach, and bring claims before they are time-barred. Contrast the whistleblower who was awarded about $20 million in June 2026 after providing information that caused the Commission to open its investigation, meeting with staff numerous times and helping them understand highly complex transactions. The same report carries a second warning about exit paperwork: Foot Locker paid a $148,000 civil penalty for violating Rule 21F-17(a) after about 148 departing employees signed separation agreements (July 2020–June 2024) that purported to waive their rights to receive SEC whistleblower awards. Two action items: tell a client with a real tip not to sit on it, because the calendar is part of the award calculation; and review separation and severance language before a client signs it, because a waiver of award rights is not just unenforceable, it can draw an enforcement action. Remember the upside: when the maximum award would be $5 million or less and there are no negative factors (culpability, unreasonable delay, interference with internal compliance), Rule 21F-6(c) presumes the whistleblower gets the maximum 30 percent.

## Tech angle

**The tip flood: about 26,000 tips arrived last year, and two people filed nearly a third of them.** In fiscal 2026 the SEC received approximately 26,000 whistleblower tips — and about 7,500 of them were attributable to just two individuals. Law360 reported on November 18, 2026 that whistleblower attorneys say artificial intelligence is behind a surge of tips, because AI makes it cheap to generate and file claims at volume. The agency's answer is analytics-led triage through its Office of Super Intelligence and Analytics, which cross-references trading data against corporate events (the ARTEMIS system) and ranks leads before a human reads them. What survives triage is specificity, documents, and staying involved: the joint whistleblowers awarded about $12 million took part in more than two dozen calls and video conferences with staff and filed nearly two dozen supplemental submissions with new analysis and information. That is a working file, not a form letter.

**AI-whistleblower watch:** On September 17, 2026, the New York Attorney General's office issued an industry alert encouraging workers with information about potentially unsafe or unlawful AI development to submit confidential whistleblower complaints through a secure portal. New York's Responsible AI Safety and Education Act (RAISE Act) takes effect January 1, 2027, and will require covered frontier AI developers to maintain safety frameworks, assess catastrophic risk, and report critical safety incidents. Attorneys with a client inside an AI company now have two doors: the federal securities route and a state AI-safety route that opens wider in January.

**Docket note:** On November 17, 2026, the Justice Department's National Fraud Enforcement Division, the Department of Veterans Affairs, and the VA Office of Inspector General announced the first coordinated national surge focused on VA benefits fraud. The surge ran September 1–November 17, 2026, brought together 17 U.S. Attorneys' Offices, and produced charges against 15 defendants in new cases involving more than $3.4 million in intended losses; guilty pleas, sentencings, and trial verdicts in the same window totaled more than $19 million. Examples: Darlene Toman of Ohio, charged with stealing $864,380 from 11 disabled veterans whose finances she managed as their fiduciary; Yolanda Johnson Sayed, 61, a prosthetics representative at the Houston VA Medical Center, charged with conspiracy to commit wire fraud in a scheme involving about $182,000 in inflated delivery fees on mobility equipment (U.S. v. Yolanda Johnson, No. 4:26-cr-699, S.D. Tex.); and Sean Lightfoot, accused of assuming the identity of a deceased veteran to access $70,000 in benefits. All defendants charged in the new cases are presumed innocent unless proven guilty; the convictions and pleas cited are separate, further-advanced matters. The department also announced a new Texas Federal Fraud Task Force, bringing together all four U.S. Attorneys' Offices in Texas.
