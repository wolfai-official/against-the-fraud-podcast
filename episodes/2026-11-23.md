## Case of the day

**Two regulators and the FBI move on the same day against an Indianapolis fund manager accused of running a $560 million Ponzi scheme sold as AI-driven currency trading (announced Nov 20, 2026).** On Friday, November 20, 2026, the Securities and Exchange Commission announced it had obtained a temporary restraining order, asset freeze, expedited discovery, and other emergency relief against Indianapolis-based Backward Hat Capital, its co-founders Phillip Williams and Devin Johnson, Johnson's company Traders Point Capital, and Joseph Anderson. The SEC's complaint was filed November 17, 2026, in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Indiana and unsealed November 20. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission filed a parallel complaint the same day against Williams, Johnson, and eleven companies it calls the Backward Hat Common Enterprise, and federal prosecutors in Manhattan filed criminal charges against both men. The FBI arrested Williams in Kokomo, Indiana, and Johnson in Gilbert, Arizona, on Wednesday, November 18.

Beginning around July 2022, Williams and Johnson allegedly raised money for algorithmic, high-frequency foreign exchange trading, telling investors their funds would be converted into crypto assets and then used for FX arbitrage run by a proprietary, "AI-driven" algorithm that always returned at least 6% per month. The SEC says the operation raised approximately $560 million from more than 2,000 investors; the CFTC puts it at more than $550 million from at least 2,100 participants, and Manhattan prosecutors put the total raised at more than $600 million. The CFTC alleges the defendants conducted little to no forex trading at all. Instead, more than $250 million went out as Ponzi-like payments to earlier investors and more than $220 million went to Williams and Johnson themselves — exotic cars, expensive homes, other unrelated businesses, and Johnson's gambling. The scheme was still running when the government moved: the CFTC says the defendants kept soliciting funds at least through October 2026, and in one example in its complaint, between September 19, 2025, and October 9, 2026, they raised around $350 million. The SEC says that since September the defendants delayed or failed to pay withdrawal requests.

Joseph Anderson is accused of creating fake financial reports for distribution to investors, including at least two "audit reports" and a "quality of earnings" report from a so-called third-party independent audit firm, falsely stating that the funds' returns had been confirmed and verified through a review of bank and brokerage records. The SEC says Anderson and his company did not perform financial audit work, were not independent, had significant financial interests in the scheme, and the reports contradicted the bank records. The Indiana court entered a restraining order on November 18 freezing the defendants' assets and on November 20 appointed Kevin B. Duff as temporary receiver. The CFTC seeks restitution, disgorgement, civil monetary penalties, trading and registration bans, and a permanent injunction; the SEC seeks permanent injunctions, disgorgement with prejudgment interest, and civil penalties. The criminal charges are conspiracy to commit wire fraud, conspiracy to commit securities fraud, and securities fraud, with a combined maximum sentence of 45 years. All of this is allegations, not findings — no defendant has been found liable in the civil cases or convicted in the criminal case.

## FCA practice note

**The CFTC's whistleblower program just had its biggest year ever — and the backlog is finally moving.** The CFTC Whistleblower Office's annual report for fiscal year 2026 (October 1, 2025–September 30, 2026) shows the Commission granted awards totaling more than $193 million and issued 28 final award determinations, the highest number in a single fiscal year since the program began. The Commission received 1,695 whistleblower tips (Form TCRs) and 107 award applications (Form WB-APPs), and issued 40 orders denying claims that did not meet the basic requirements. Since the program issued its first award in 2014, it has now paid more than $580 million to whistleblowers, and the program has helped return $4.5 billion in penalties to the U.S. Treasury. Ten of the awards, totaling more than $150 million, were announced in September 2026 alone, covering determinations issued between July and September. The Commission's general counsel said the office had inherited a substantial backlog and had moved to process awards promptly and efficiently.

For attorneys, three practical takeaways. First, the backlog point matters: if a client held off filing because the program looked stalled, that calculation has changed — 28 determinations in one year is the proof. Second, the money is concentrated: ten awards accounted for more than $150 million of the $193 million total, so a tip tied to a large, successful enforcement action is where the award lives, and the Commission does not disclose which action or how much any individual received, to protect confidentiality. Third, remember the September 11, 2026 final rule that presumes a 30% award — the maximum — when the largest possible award is $5 million or less and the whistleblower meets the rule's conditions. But volume is not a strategy: in one award determination issued the same day as the rule (No. 26-WB-25), the Commission reduced an award, saying past large awards had produced a flood of frivolous tip filings and award applications that drained the office's resources. A specific, credible, timely tip is what counts. Awards run 10–30% of monetary sanctions collected, and only when sanctions exceed $1 million.

## Tech angle

**How the "AI trading" pitch unraveled: bank and brokerage records, not the algorithm, exposed a trading strategy that barely traded at all.** The forensic checks are straightforward. First, the brokerage statements: a fund trading foreign exchange generates execution, counterparty, and settlement records, and the CFTC says there was little to no trading — the absence of those records is itself the evidence. Second, the bank records: the fake audit reports claimed the returns had been confirmed and verified through a review of bank and brokerage records, but the SEC says the reports contradicted the bank records. An auditor who is not independent, who has a financial interest in the fund, and whose reports disagree with the bank statements is not an auditor — he is a prop. Third, the flow of funds: more than $250 million recycled to earlier investors, more than $220 million to the founders, and around $350 million raised in about a year while withdrawal requests were being delayed or denied. A fund that pays 6% a month, every month, while its founders buy exotic cars and its investors cannot get their money out, is not trading — it is redistributing. Fourth, the cross-border trail: the CFTC thanked regulators in Lithuania, British Columbia, the Cayman Islands, Cyprus, and Singapore for their assistance, which shows the money moved across borders and the regulators followed it there. The lesson for attorneys: when a client brings a fund promising AI-driven returns, start with the records — brokerage statements, bank statements, and audit reports, and who did the audit and whether that firm is independent. The algorithm is the marketing. The records are the case.
