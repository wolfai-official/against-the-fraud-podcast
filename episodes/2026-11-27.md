## Case of the day

**A New York judge orders the NovaTech founders to pay more than $1.3 billion for a crypto Ponzi scheme that targeted Haitian communities (judgment signed Nov 24, 2026; announced Nov 25).** In the New York Attorney General's case against NovaTechFx (NovaTech), New York Supreme Court Justice Anar Rathod Patel signed a monetary judgment ordering founders Cynthia and Eddy Petion — together with Nova Tech Ltd., NovaTech Advisors LLC and NovaPay LLC — to pay $1,367,023,585, jointly and severally. The award comprises $1,134,549,691 in disgorgement and $232,473,894 in prejudgment interest at nine percent a year. The state alleged NovaTech promised weekly returns of two to four percent from cryptocurrency and foreign-exchange trading, when almost none of that trading ever happened; new investor money paid earlier investors. The Petions specifically targeted Haitian communities, promoting the scheme in the Creole language and through faith groups and community organizations. The judgment covers hundreds of thousands of investors worldwide, including 11,000 New Yorkers, and the state's accounting says investors deposited $1,160,544,294 in cryptocurrency into NovaTech wallets. The SEC's separate federal case, filed August 12, 2024, alleged NovaTech raised more than $650 million from over 200,000 investors between 2019 and 2023, with only a small fraction ever actually traded. The Attorney General sued on June 6, 2024 under New York's Martin Act and Executive Law section 63(12); the Petions fled the country and never answered, so the court entered a default judgment on August 4, 2025 finding them liable and permanently banning them from the securities, commodities and cryptocurrency industries. This week's order puts a number on that liability. A judgment is an order to pay, not money recovered: the founders' whereabouts remain unknown, and the state has not said how much can be collected or returned to victims.

**Second story: A father and son plead guilty in a $600 million COVID tax-credit scheme (DOJ announced Nov 24, 2026, E.D.N.Y.).** Keith Williams, 48, and his son Jamari Lewis, 28, of West Hempstead, New York, pleaded guilty for their roles in a conspiracy that filed more than 8,000 false employment tax returns claiming COVID-19-related employment tax credits. Operating through a purported credit repair business Williams owned, Credit Reset, and acting as tax preparers, they claimed two pandemic programs: the Employee Retention Credit and the paid sick and family leave credit. The conspirators sought more than $600 million in fraudulent credits, of which the IRS paid approximately $46 million. Both men agreed to pay restitution to the IRS of $46,756,084.89; Williams also agreed to forfeit $4,848,998.44 and Lewis $671,511.11. Williams pleaded guilty on November 24 to one count of conspiracy to defraud the United States and one count of wire fraud (up to five years on the conspiracy count, up to thirty years on wire fraud); Lewis pleaded guilty on November 19 to one count of wire fraud (up to thirty years) and is scheduled to be sentenced on April 19, 2027. Prosecutors have called it the largest Employee Retention Credit fraud case in the country. The case was investigated by IRS Criminal Investigation, the U.S. Postal Inspection Service and Homeland Security Investigations.

## FCA practice note

**The CFTC just started a ninety-day whistleblower award clock — calendar the notice date, not the judgment date.** On November 24, 2026, the CFTC posted Notice of Covered Action No. 2026-008, covering the enforcement action against Michael Frederick Staryk, who did business as Magestic World Wide Finance. A federal judge in Connecticut entered a default judgment against Staryk on September 14, 2026 (24-CV-1561 (OAW), D. Conn.), ordering $547,616 in restitution to defrauded clients and a $5,907,720 civil monetary penalty, plus permanent trading and registration bans. The CFTC alleged Staryk fraudulently solicited funds from at least 26 retail clients, supposedly to trade options on commodity futures, and took about $600,000 between 2021 and 2022; the trading never happened, and he was already permanently banned from trading when he did it. Relief defendants Yvonne Stephanie Solerti-Coto and Global Financial Institution LLC were separately ordered to disgorge $110,509.86 in client funds.

The award clock does not start at the judgment — it starts when the CFTC posts the notice of covered action. Anyone who gave the CFTC original information that led to this enforcement action now has ninety days to file a Form WB-APP award application. The deadline is February 22, 2027, and after that date the claim is gone. Eligible awards run ten to thirty percent of the sanctions the CFTC collects. The trap for new filers: the notice itself says the CFTC has not determined that any tip led to the action, or that an award will be paid. The notice only opens the window; the application has to tie the client's information to the enforcement action, and it has to be filed before February 22. The SEC's version of this ninety-day deadline works the same way, and the same discipline applies.

## Tech angle

**The FBI caught a ransomware recovery firm secretly paying hackers — by playing both sides of the deal.** Juan Ricardo Palacio Escobar, 52, a Colombian national who co-founded a Florida data recovery company and served as its chief technology officer, was indicted on November 18, 2026 by a federal grand jury in the District of Rhode Island on three counts of wire fraud (United States v. Juan Ricardo Palacio Escobar, No. 1:26-cr-00096-MRD-RLA; announced November 25). The company, identified in reporting as RansomwareHelp, marketed itself to ransomware victims with the promise that it never negotiated with hackers and never paid ransoms, claiming proprietary decryption technology could recover encrypted files on its own. Prosecutors allege the company had no such technology: instead, Palacio secretly negotiated with ransomware actors, paid them for decryption keys, and charged victims substantially more, keeping the difference. The scheme allegedly ran from about October 2020 to June 2026, and prosecutors say he used the proceeds to buy several properties in Florida.

The FBI proved it with an undercover operation in June 2026 in which agents posed as both sides of the transaction: a company supposedly hit by ransomware, and the fictitious ransomware gang behind it. Palacio's firm repeated its no-ransom pitch to the fake victim, then Palacio negotiated with the fake gang — first quoted about $13,000 — haggled them down, and paid about $6,000 in Bitcoin for the decryption key. He then charged the fake victim company about $13,260 for his supposed decryption technology, plus a $2,750 diagnostic fee, for a total of about $16,010. Because the FBI held both wallets and both sides of the conversation, every step was documented. The broader lesson: Bitcoin payments settle on a public ledger that anyone can inspect — they are pseudonymous, not anonymous — and a recovery firm's ransom payment leaves a permanent, traceable record. Palacio was arrested in Las Vegas on October 22 and released on pretrial conditions on November 20. He faces up to twenty years on each count. An indictment is only an allegation, and he is presumed innocent unless convicted.
