## Case of the day

**The CFTC pays more than $11 million to 14 whistleblowers for one case — and will not say which case (announced Nov 23, 2026).** On Monday, November 23, 2026, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission announced it is awarding more than $11 million to 14 whistleblowers whose original information led to a successful enforcement action against an extensive fraudulent scheme. The Commission will not name the case, the whistleblowers, or how much any one of them received — confidentiality is required by the Commodity Exchange Act, which protects whistleblower identity, and the Commission does not disclose information that could reasonably be expected to reveal an informant.

Raagnee Beri, director of the CFTC's Whistleblower Office, said the awardees "supported an unusually challenging investigation and lawsuit" and "distinguished themselves from dozens of other witnesses to the defendants' wrongdoing." David I. Miller, director of the Enforcement Division, said each whistleblower "provided critical information that helped the CFTC stop the subject fraud and recover investor losses." Tyler Badgley, the Commission's general counsel, said "These awards continue a record year for rewarding meritorious whistleblowers." The Commission also denied six other award claims in the same matter — claimants who failed to provide supporting documents or whose information was not significant to the underlying action.

The program's scale: since its first award in 2014, the CFTC has awarded whistleblowers more than $590 million in connection with enforcement actions that produced more than $5.1 billion in monetary sanctions. Eligible whistleblowers can receive 10–30% of the sanctions collected, paid from the Customer Protection Fund, which Congress established and which is funded entirely through sanctions paid by violators — not from harmed customers. Context: the program's fiscal year 2026 annual report, covered in yesterday's episode, showed more than $193 million in awards and 28 final award determinations, the most in a single year; and in September 2026 the Commission granted multiple awards totaling more than $150 million in one round. Tips are filed electronically on Form TCR.

**Second story: two North Carolina tax preparers sentenced in a $23.9 million pandemic-credit scheme (Nov 23, 2026).** Whitnee Leach, office manager of a tax preparation business in Lumberton and Hope Mills, North Carolina, was sentenced to 43 months in prison; Jeannie Negron, a preparer at the business, was sentenced to six months. From approximately April 2022 through May 2023, Leach, business owner Nejlai Mitchell, and six employees filed false tax returns seeking fraudulent refunds based on the paid sick and family leave credit, a credit Congress passed to aid struggling businesses during the pandemic. The conspiracy produced approximately $23.9 million in fraudulent refund claims, more than $13 million of which the IRS paid out. Leach was ordered to pay $13,890,697 in restitution and serve three years of supervised release; Negron was ordered to pay $73,514 in restitution and serve one year of supervised release. Leach and Mitchell pleaded guilty to conspiracy to defraud the IRS and to helping clients file false tax returns; the other six pleaded guilty to helping clients file false returns. Mitchell was sentenced October 1 to 60 months; five other preparers were sentenced earlier in 2026 (Moody 26 months, Ray 18, McBurney 18, Swindell 16, Stanback six). The announcement came from Assistant Attorney General Colin M. McDonald of the National Fraud Enforcement Division and U.S. Attorney W. Ellis Boyle for the Eastern District of North Carolina. The case is part of the Justice Department's first-ever National Tax Enforcement Surge: actions against 76 defendants with an estimated intended loss of more than $490 million (23 new-charge actions against 31 defendants, $350 million-plus; around 39 other actions affecting 45 defendants, nearly $140 million).

## FCA practice note

**Four Fifth Circuit judges now question whether qui tam violates the Constitution — and the chief judge says the question is "no doubt en banc worthy."** On November 13, 2026, the Fifth Circuit affirmed the dismissal of Brook Jackson's qui tam suit against Ventavia Research Group, which operated clinical trial sites for Pfizer's COVID-19 vaccine trials (No. 24-40564). The government declined to intervene during the seal period, then moved to intervene and dismiss under 31 U.S.C. § 3730(c)(2)(A); the district court granted the motion, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed under the Supreme Court's 2023 decision in Polansky, applying Rule 41. It also affirmed dismissal of Jackson's retaliation claim. The dismissal holding itself was covered in the November 14 episode; the development is what two judges wrote alongside it.

Judge Don R. Willett, who wrote the majority opinion, also wrote a lengthy concurrence explaining why the qui tam device gives him "serious constitutional pause." He grounded his concerns in the Appointments Clause, the Vesting Clause, and the Take Care Clause. On the Appointments Clause, he called whether a relator occupies a continuing position established by law a "closer question," but found the condition satisfied, reasoning that a relator who may litigate a single case for years holds something more than a temporary role. He described the Vesting and Take Care Clauses as presenting "a distinct — and stronger — problem": in his view, more recent Supreme Court decisions preclude Congress from delegating enforcement power to private relators without appropriate supervision, which the FCA lacks. He refuted the standard defenses: the government's power to intervene and dismiss cannot qualify as adequate control because "control that must be reclaimed is not control"; founding-era history cannot preserve the provisions because "pedigree is not permission"; treating the relator as an assignee may transfer a claim but cannot transfer executive authority.

Chief Judge Jennifer Walker Elrod wrote briefly to endorse those concerns as "no doubt en banc worthy," adding that "it may be time for our en banc court to revisit" the court's 2001 en banc decision in Riley v. St. Luke's Episcopal Hospital, 252 F.3d 749 (5th Cir. 2001), upholding the qui tam provisions. Willett and Elrod join Judges Ho and Duncan, who raised similar concerns in 2025 concurrences — four Fifth Circuit judges in writing. If the full court revisits and overturns Riley, it would create a circuit split: five circuits (Fifth, Sixth, Ninth, Tenth, Eleventh) have rejected Appointments Clause challenges, the Eleventh most recently in Zafirov (Sept 1, 2026), followed by the summary reversal in Berry v. Native American Services (Nov 12, 2026). Arguments that the qui tam provisions violate Article II remain pending in several other circuit and district courts, making it likely the issue reaches the Supreme Court in the appropriate case.

For relators' counsel: expect defendants to raise Article II challenges in every qui tam case they can, citing Willett by name; preserve the constitutional response now, because an en banc petition is no longer remote — the chief judge has invited it in writing. And remember what is at stake: if the qui tam device fell, the government would lose the mechanism that produces the overwhelming majority of its False Claims Act recoveries.

## Tech angle

**How the IRS caught the tax-fraud surge cases with no whistleblower at all: return-level data matching.** In the North Carolina scheme and the other surge cases, the fraud was found the same way it was proved — by matching one set of records against another. Claimed credits against employer filings. Claimed wages against W-2 records. Refund destinations against taxpayer addresses. Claim volume against a single preparer.

The patterns: Marty Allen Linville (W.D. Ark.) gave employees W-2 forms reporting withholdings but never filed those W-2s with the Social Security Administration or made the required deposits; employees found their refunds frozen because no W-2 was on file with the IRS. He was sentenced to 20 months with $3.8 million in restitution. Sherita Booker (N.D. Ohio) allegedly prepared false returns for hundreds of clients using the same three programs — COVID-19 sick leave credits for non-existent businesses and employees, fuel tax refunds, and overpaid-tax claims. Four men in Texas (Rojano Salazar, N.D. Ga.) obtained taxpayer identification numbers using Mexican identification documents in other people's names, filed fraudulent returns in those names, and directed refunds to post office boxes they controlled — more than 16,000 Treasury refund checks worth over $74 million, cashed through a single check-cashing company. John Labee (W.D. Tex.) allegedly filed nine false returns for a business with minimal operations and no employees, claiming the Employee Retention Credit and sick and family leave credit on fabricated wage figures — approximately $6.9 million sought, more than $1.1 million paid.

The contrast with the case of the day: at the CFTC, the case started with a human being who filed a tip electronically on Form TCR. At the IRS, the cases started with the data disagreeing with itself. Both paths end in the same place — a paper trail the defendant cannot explain away. One caution: detection by matching works after the money moves. In North Carolina, more than $13 million went out before the sentencings; in the Labee case, more than $1.1 million was paid. A person inside the room can stop a fraud before the checks are cut; a database can only catch it after.
