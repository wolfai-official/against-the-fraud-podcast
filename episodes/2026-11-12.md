## Case of the day

**Dana-Farber Cancer Institute — $15M research-fraud settlement (announced Dec 16, 2025).**
Dana-Farber agreed to pay $15 million to resolve False Claims Act allegations that, from 2014 through 2024, it made materially false statements and certifications tied to National Institutes of Health research grants. In the settlement agreement, Dana-Farber admitted its researchers used funds from six NIH grants to conduct research that produced 14 scientific publications containing misrepresented and/or duplicated images — images reused to represent different experimental conditions, duplicated for different testing conditions, mice, or timepoints, or rotated, magnified, or stretched. Dana-Farber also admitted another researcher received four NIH grants after submitting applications that cited a journal article without disclosing that images and data in it were misrepresented or duplicated. $8,571,428.57 of the settlement is restitution. The claims are allegations only with no determination of liability, but the settlement agreement contains the institute's factual admissions. The qui tam case, *U.S. ex rel. Sholto David v. Dana-Farber Cancer Institute, Inc.*, No. 2:24-cv-11059-WGY (D. Mass.), was filed April 22, 2024 by Sholto David, a Welsh molecular biologist whose Jan 2, 2024 "Dana-Farberications" blog post on For Better Science flagged image problems across dozens of Dana-Farber papers. David receives $2,625,000 (17.5% of the recovery). Dana-Farber retracted several papers and corrected about 30 more after the blog post. No new DOJ FCA settlement had landed in the two days before this episode, so the show caught up on this major research-fraud case it had not yet covered.

## FCA practice note

**First-to-file is unforgiving: *U.S. ex rel. 3DQA LLC v. Theken Companies* (M.D. Fla. 2026).** The court dismissed with prejudice a qui tam action alleging medical-device maker Theken Companies funneled kickbacks to surgeons disguised as product-development royalties, with relators claiming over $118 million in FCA damages and penalties. The first-to-file rule barred the fraud claim because an earlier-filed action (*United States ex rel. Fries v. NextStep Arthropedix*, No. 2:22-cv-98) alleged substantially the same scheme — filed the very same day, Feb 14, 2022. The court: "The FCA is clear: 'if you ain't first, you're last.'" (*Talladega Nights*.) The court also rejected the relators' FCA retaliation claim: the two individual relators (former employees Michael Rappach and Bobi Lekic) alleged the defendants caused criminal charges to be filed against them for stealing company records, but they did not file their qui tam until about four years after their employment ended, and the court held the FCA anti-retaliation provision does not cover post-employment conduct. Takeaways: run a docket check the same week you file, move fast once a client walks in, and do not assume the FCA covers retaliation years after employment ends.

## Tech angle

Research fraud is caught by image forensics: a sleuth compares figures across papers by eye, then uses automated screening (David has discussed Imagetwin) that searches an image against a library of published figures and flags whether it has appeared before under a different caption. Journals and publishers now run automated duplication screening before publication. The same image-matching approach generalizes to any fraud case — the file itself is the evidence.

**AI-whistleblower watch:** On Oct 1, 2026, OpenAI confirmed it parted ways with three safety researchers after an internal investigation found they mishandled sensitive company information; the researchers dispute that account and say the firings followed their safety concerns. The *Wall Street Journal* identified them as Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak, and Mikita Balesni (OpenAI has not named them). On Oct 29, 2026, 20 House Democrats wrote to OpenAI demanding answers, with a response due Nov 13. On Oct 30, 2026, OpenAI published a new Raising Concerns Policy covering anonymous reporting of AI safety concerns without retaliation. On Nov 6, 2026, the independent Lab Canary project launched, rating AI labs on whistleblower protections from public evidence only: 35 labs tracked, 18 with no public information, and the top scores — Anthropic and OpenAI — at just 43%.
