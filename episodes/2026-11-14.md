## Case of the day

**Johnson Controls Inc. and two subsidiaries — $5M cybersecurity settlement (announced Nov 13, 2026).**
Johnson Controls, Inc. of Milwaukee, Wisconsin, and subsidiaries Johnson Controls Fire Protection LP and Johnson Controls Security Solutions LLC agreed to pay $5 million to resolve False Claims Act allegations that they failed to comply with cybersecurity requirements in federal contracts and subcontracts for building products and services, including heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) services. The alleged conduct ran from January 2018 through August 2025, covering contracts with NASA and the Departments of Defense, Energy, and Justice. The government alleged two failures: the companies failed to control access to protected information, because they lacked sufficient information about their own information systems and sufficient control over those systems; and, on contracts requiring compliance with NIST Special Publication 800-171, they failed to maintain a System Security Plan meeting contractual requirements and failed to adequately document, fully develop, or fully implement a plan of action and milestones to correct deficiencies and reduce or eliminate vulnerabilities. No data breach is alleged. The claims are allegations only with no determination of liability. The settlement resolves two qui tam suits by former employees: *United States ex rel. Kelly Vance v. Johnson Controls, Inc.*, No. 1:24-cv-2500 (N.D. Ga.), and *United States ex rel. David Thomas v. Johnson Controls Int'l PLC*, No. 1:26-cv-448 (D.D.C.). Vance receives $850,000 as his share; no share was stated for Thomas. Investigated by the DOJ Civil Division, the U.S. Attorneys' Offices for the Northern District of Georgia and the District of Columbia, Defense Criminal Investigative Service, NCIS, Air Force Office of Special Investigations, and the inspectors general of NASA, Energy, Justice, and GSA. Acting Assistant Attorney General Jonathan Guynn spoke for the Civil Division — the first major FCA announcement under acting leadership after Brett Shumate resigned Nov 9, 2026 (covered Nov 11).

## FCA practice note

**The government's power to end a qui tam case: Fifth Circuit affirms dismissal in *Brook Jackson v. Ventavia* (Nov 13, 2026).** Brook Jackson worked under three weeks in September 2020 as a regional director overseeing two vaccine testing sites run by Ventavia Research Group, a contractor on Pfizer's COVID-19 vaccine clinical trial. She alleged she witnessed trial-protocol and FDA-regulation violations, reported them to Ventavia management and then to the FDA, and was fired September 25, 2020, the same day she went to the FDA. She filed a qui tam suit in January 2021 alleging falsified and unreliable trial data induced the FDA's emergency use authorization and the government's payments to Pfizer. The government investigated and declined to intervene in January 2022, expressly reserving its right to intervene later for good cause, then moved to intervene for the sole purpose of dismissing the case; the district court in the Eastern District of Texas (No. 1:21-cv-00008-MJT, Judge Truncale) agreed. On Friday, November 13, 2026, the Fifth Circuit affirmed, in an opinion by Judge Don Willett, holding the trial court acted within its discretion. The opinion turned on the Supreme Court's 2023 decision in *United States ex rel. Polansky v. Executive Health Resources Inc.* Takeaways for relators' counsel: (1) when DOJ moves to dismiss, the court reviews with a light touch — Polansky lets the government dismiss over a relator's objection on a reasonable basis; (2) the late-intervention / good-cause fight is uphill — the statute allows later intervention for good cause and courts read that generously when the government wants to dismiss; (3) build the retaliation record early and separately — Jackson also appealed the dismissal of her retaliation claim, and that theory lives or dies on contemporaneous documentation of protected activity, timing, and who knew what when. Sourcing note: the affirmance itself was reported by Bloomberg Law on Nov 13 (the opinion is fresh); the procedural history, dates, and legal framework come from the public court record, including the government's appellate brief and the district court's published dismissal order.

## Tech angle

Cyber False Claims Act fraud is proved by paperwork, not by a breach. Investigators compare what the contract required against what the company actually had: the contract, the System Security Plan, the plan of action and milestones, and the assessment scores. When the documents stop matching — access controls undocumented, a security plan that does not meet the requirements, a fix-it list never finished — the company's own files supply the case, with no need to show a hacker got in. Two insider relators made this case, filing two separate suits. And the pattern is now unmistakable: on November 3, 2026, ILC Dover and ILC Astrospace agreed to pay $13 million — the largest cyber-fraud recovery under DOJ's Civil Cybersecurity Fraud Initiative (launched October 2021) — on the same theory, the same NIST 800-171 controls, and the same kind of missing System Security Plan (covered Nov 9). Ten days later, Johnson Controls added $5 million. Two settlements, $18 million combined, both built on what the paperwork did, or did not, say. For contractors, the lesson is that a System Security Plan and a plan of action and milestones are not formalities: under the False Claims Act, those documents are the evidence. For investigators, the method is to start with the contract and ask at each step whether the next document matches the one before it.
