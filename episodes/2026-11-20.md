## Case of the day

**Ruby Scott / Delta Home Health Care LLC sentenced to four years in prison for a ~$1.6 million Medicare fraud scheme fueled by illegal kickbacks (sentenced Nov 19, 2026, E.D. Mich.).** Ruby Scott, 55, of Farmington Hills, Michigan, owned and operated Delta Home Health Care LLC. From 2018 through 2021, Scott bribed a discharge nurse at a Detroit hospital to identify Medicare patients and fax their confidential records to Delta, unbeknownst to the patients. She paid the nurse between $270 and $300 per patient — more than $130,000 in total, sent by CashApp, PayPal, check, and cash. Scott used the stolen patient profiles to bill Medicare for home health services, including for patients who never received any services from Delta, and falsely represented to Medicare that a doctor had certified patients as homebound when no doctor had ever evaluated them. Delta failed to maintain patient files for more than a third of the patients it billed Medicare for, and Medicare paid Delta more than $1.2 million on those claims. In total, Scott caused approximately $1.6 million in losses to Medicare. This was a criminal conviction, not a settlement: a jury convicted Scott in May 2026 on five counts of health care fraud, one count of conspiracy to defraud the United States and pay illegal health care kickbacks, and four counts of paying illegal health care kickbacks. Along with the four-year sentence, she was ordered to pay $1,652,130.67 in restitution to Medicare and the same amount in forfeiture. Attorney takeaways: kickbacks paid through payment apps leave a dated, timestamped trail, and the absence of records — no patient file, no doctor evaluation, no homebound certification — can itself be the proof of fraud.

## FCA practice note

**Regeneron: the causation standard now depends on which theory the government stands on.** On November 19, 2026, a Massachusetts federal judge ruled that prosecutors do not need to prove Regeneron Pharmaceuticals was the "but-for" cause of providers presenting alleged false claims for its macular degeneration drug Eylea, holding that a more relaxed causation standard applies to the government's false-certification theory. Background: the government alleges Regeneron paid more than $60 million over about five years to the Chronic Disease Fund, a charitable foundation, to subsidize patient copays for Eylea with the intent of inducing prescriptions and improperly billing Medicare; Regeneron disputes the payments were kickbacks, and the claims are allegations only. The fight turns on the Anti-Kickback Statute language added in 2010, under which a claim that includes items or services "resulting from" a kickback violation counts as a false claim under the FCA. Judge F. Dennis Saylor held "resulting from" means but-for causation and granted Regeneron partial summary judgment; the First Circuit agreed on February 18, 2026, joining the Sixth and Eighth Circuits, while the Third Circuit requires only a "link" between the kickback and the claim. That ruling narrowed the case, so on August 4, 2026 the district court allowed the government to pursue an alternate false-certification theory — that Regeneron falsely certified compliance with the anti-kickback law and that the certification was material to the government's payment decision — and reopened discovery. The November 19 ruling holds the relaxed standard applies to that theory. Inside the same courthouse, Judge Saylor and Judge Nathaniel Gorton have now reached opposite conclusions on causation (Saylor in Regeneron, Gorton in the Teva case), deepening a split that makes Supreme Court review more likely. Practical point: in a kickback-based FCA case, plead both theories, because the causation burden depends on the theory; for defense counsel, the false-certification theory is the one to watch, because it may let the government around the but-for wall the First Circuit built.

## Tech angle

**How the fraud is found: the paper trail, now scanned by software.** The Scott case was built from records: fax logs showing patient files leaving a hospital, payment-app transfers of $270–$300 at a time adding up to more than $130,000, and Medicare claims for patients with no files and no doctor evaluations. The Veterans Affairs Department is applying the same idea at scale. It is building a tool that uses Microsoft's Power Business Intelligence analytics program to scan more than a million Disability Benefits Questionnaires (DBQs) filed since 2010, flagging high-volume submissions from the same provider, boilerplate language repeated across veterans, signs a document was altered, and an examiner located more than 100 miles from the veteran — the fingerprints of "DBQ mills," companies that pose as legitimate medical providers and file claims on veterans' behalf while charging excessive fees. The VA Inspector General's January 2024 report found that of nearly 32,000 claims completed in 2022, 69% contained one or more indicators of potential fraud risk, with an estimated value of about $390 million; over the past ten years the department has sent cease-and-desist letters to at least 40 companies. Guardrail: the department says the tool will not be used against past claims — older questionnaires train the system, which is aimed at new claims, and no veteran's benefit will be reduced or denied because of the effort. For attorneys: if you represent a provider or claims company, volume, language, and distance are now measurable before anyone is interviewed; if you represent a whistleblower, those same patterns are what make a tip specific enough to survive triage.

**Docket note:** On November 19, 2026, a Massachusetts federal judge pressed pause on a massive qui tam lawsuit alleging pandemic relief fraud by 120 nonprofit organizations, asking the relator to explain how it expects to prove intent, get around the public-disclosure bar, and satisfy joinder requirements for suing that many defendants at once. Context: in September 2026, a similar complaint was unsealed in Maryland (*United States ex rel. The Tarbell Group, LLC v. American Academy of Environmental Engineers and Scientists Inc., et al.*, No. MJM-25-cv-915 (D. Md.)), naming more than 70 Maryland tax-exempt organizations over Paycheck Protection Program loans of $5,000–$2.5 million. The data-built, multi-defendant qui tam is facing its first real test in court, and rulings on intent and joinder will decide whether the model survives.

**Numbers note:** The Commodity Futures Trading Commission awarded more than $193 million to whistleblowers in fiscal 2026, across 28 final award determinations — the highest number ever in a single fiscal year — after receiving 1,695 tips. A record year, following the Commission's rule (effective October 16, 2026) presuming the maximum 30 percent award when the award would be $5 million or less.
