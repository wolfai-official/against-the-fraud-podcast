## Case of the day

**Michael Bacon v. SEC — Wells Fargo whistleblower fights a $125M award cut in the Third Circuit (brief filed Nov 6, 2026).**
Michael Bacon, Wells Fargo's former chief security officer, gave the government information about the fake-accounts scandal — employees opening millions of accounts customers never asked for between 2002 and 2016 — that helped build enforcement cases ending in Wells Fargo's $3 billion February 2020 settlement with the DOJ and the SEC ($500 million of it an SEC civil penalty). Under the Dodd-Frank Act, a whistleblower whose tip leads to a qualifying enforcement action gets 10–30% of the sanctions collected. The SEC's Claims Review Staff recommended a $179.5 million award on July 8, 2024; Bacon accepted it on July 25, 2024, though he believed it understated his entitlement. After Paul Atkins became SEC chair on April 21, 2025, the agency issued a superseding preliminary determination on May 5, 2025, cutting the award to $54.5 million — a drop of about 70% — and adopted the cut in a final order on April 7, 2026. On November 6, 2026, Bacon's attorney H. Vincent McKnight filed a 70-page brief in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit (John Doe v. SEC, Nos. 26-2493 and 26-1981), arguing the award violates the 10% statutory floor: the brief says the final award works out to about 1.7% of the sanctions collected. Bacon asks the court to order an award of about $300 million or, alternatively, restore the $179.5 million preliminary award. The brief describes years of cooperation with federal and state investigators and argues the reversal followed the change in SEC leadership. The SEC has not yet responded in court; its response is due December 21, 2026, with oral argument expected in 2027. Context: the nonprofit Better Markets reported in March 2026 that the SEC awarded about $60 million in fiscal 2025, down from more than $100 million in each of the five prior years, and denied far more claims. Bacon's allegations are his side of a pending appeal; the SEC's position has not yet been filed.

## FCA practice note

**Jarkesy reaches the Administrative False Claims Act: *Smith v. U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development*, No. 1:25-cv-00016 (D. Guam Oct. 29, 2026).** Smith was legal counsel to the Guam Housing and Urban Renewal Authority, Guam's public housing agency, while also owning rental properties in HUD's Section 8 Housing Choice Voucher Program. HUD pursued him administratively under the statute formerly known as the Program Fraud Civil Remedies Act, now the Administrative False Claims Act (AFCA), and an administrative judge imposed a $735,372 award in penalties and assessments. The District Court of Guam vacated the award, holding that the AFCA's civil penalty and assessment provisions violate the Seventh Amendment right to a jury trial, applying the Supreme Court's 2024 decision in *SEC v. Jarkesy*, 603 U.S. 109 (2024). Cautions: this is a single district court decision and does not invalidate the AFCA nationwide; a federal court in Ohio reached a different result in a similar housing case (*Millennia Housing Management v. HUD*, 783 F. Supp. 3d 1051 (N.D. Ohio 2025)). The stakes are rising because Congress, in the FY 2025 defense authorization act, renamed the statute the AFCA and raised the maximum amount per individual claim from $150,000 to $1 million — the law exists for small-dollar fraud too expensive to litigate in federal court. Takeaways: (1) respondents in AFCA proceedings now have a citable Seventh Amendment defense, at least in Guam; (2) the government and relators should expect this challenge in every administrative case and weigh whether the case belongs in federal court instead; (3) watch for an appeal and a possible circuit split on whether Jarkesy reaches this statute. Sourcing note: the decision was flagged by a Seyfarth Shaw client alert (Nov 3, 2026), also carried on JD Supra; the underlying decision is cited at 2026 WL 3251707.

## Tech angle

The Wells Fargo fake-accounts scandal was proved by data, not by a confession. Between 2002 and 2016 the bank pushed employees to meet aggressive sales goals; thousands opened accounts customers never asked for, sometimes creating false records or misusing customers' personal information. The fraud surfaced because the records did not match: regulators fined the bank $185 million in September 2016 after finding about two million unauthorized accounts, and an independent review then raised the estimate to roughly 3.5 million accounts. The bank admitted it collected millions of dollars in fees it was not entitled to, harmed some customers' credit ratings, and misused sensitive personal information. The same data question now decides Bacon's award: the SEC's rules pay whistleblowers on sanctions collected in the covered action and in related actions based on the same information, so the Third Circuit fight turns in part on which enforcement actions count as related and how much of the $3 billion traces back to Bacon's tip. Lesson for attorneys: treat the tip as an evidence package from day one — dates, documents, the specific accounts or records, and a clear line from the information to the enforcement action — because awards can swing by more than $100 million on that matching exercise.

**Docket note:** On November 13, 2026, Chad Monroe, 48, of Tarpon Springs, Florida, pleaded guilty to a $42 million fraud on Medicare involving durable medical equipment and cancer genetic testing. Prosecutors say Monroe and co-conspirators opened three companies — Maksix Medical Products Inc., Orthotech Atlanta, LLC, and 1st Choice Medical Supply LLC — and submitted more than $26 million in false claims to Medicare, Medicare Advantage plans, and TRICARE for braces and genetic testing beneficiaries did not want or need, paying illegal kickbacks to telemedicine companies for doctors' orders. Monroe also pleaded guilty to fraudulently obtaining a Paycheck Protection Program loan. He faces up to 10 years in prison on the health care fraud conspiracy and up to 30 years on the wire fraud charge; sentencing has not been set.
