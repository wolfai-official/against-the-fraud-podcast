## Case of the day

**A federal judge in Pennsylvania lets a whistleblower's Buy American case against Philips move forward — Chinese-made CPAPs sold to the VA (opinion issued Nov 24, 2026, reported Nov 25).** In *United States ex rel. Veteran Marketing LLC v. Respironics, Inc.*, No. 2:21-cv-00853 (E.D. Pa.), Chief Judge Wendy Beetlestone denied most of Philips RS North America LLC's (formerly Respironics, Inc.) motion to dismiss. The relator, Veteran Marketing LLC, alleges the company sold the Department of Veterans Affairs CPAP masks and machines manufactured in China, in violation of the Buy American Act and the Trade Agreements Act. The masks allegedly arrived from China in boxes marked "Made in China," were assembled by hand in about ten to fifteen seconds without machines or tools — the opinion compares it to snapping a few Lego blocks together — then were repackaged in different boxes without the marking and shipped to the VA. The blowers, the core component of a CPAP machine, were also allegedly manufactured in China. Sales ran through distributor First Nation Group, LLC under two Federal Supply Schedule contracts, one from 2010 and one from December 2018. Internal documents described the VA as the company's "highest margin account," and 2018 emails from Director of Sales Paul Evans and Government Specialist Scott Mathson warned the sales team not to ship samples directly to the VA because of the "Made in China" packaging. The government did not intervene, so the relator is litigating on its own. Counts under 31 U.S.C. 3729(a)(1)(A)-(B) survive: under the Third Circuit's Rule 9(b) standard, a relator need not plead every allegedly false claim, only particular details of a scheme plus reliable indicia that claims were submitted. The reverse false claim count under (a)(1)(G) was dismissed as redundant — no affirmative obligation to pay the government was alleged. All allegations only; the court accepted the complaint's facts as true solely for the motion to dismiss, and there has been no finding of liability.

**Second story: YDH Express and affiliates pay $300,000 and are permanently barred from the mail over counterfeit postage labels (Nov 25, 2026, E.D.N.Y.).** A court-ordered settlement with Yizhao Hou and two companies he controls, YDH Express Inc. and YDH Int'l Inc., resolves a lawsuit the United States filed in May 2025 alleging the defendants shipped thousands of parcels through the U.S. Postal Service bearing counterfeit USPS labels received from their customers in China, violating the False Claims Act and the Anti-Fraud Injunction Act. The government obtained a temporary restraining order on May 16, 2025 and a preliminary injunction on June 6, 2025. The defendants will pay $300,000, provide certain financial information, and permanently stop mailing packages through USPS. E.D.N.Y. Docket No. 25-CV-2719 (NCM). The claims are allegations only, with no determination of liability.

## FCA practice note

**A district judge in the Third Circuit rejects the Article II constitutional attack on qui tam — expect the motion, and have the answer ready.** Philips argued the FCA's qui tam provisions violate Article II of the Constitution under the Appointments Clause, the Vesting Clause, and the Take Care Clause, on the theory that a private relator exercises core executive power without presidential supervision or removal. Judge Beetlestone rejected the challenge in full. On the Appointments Clause, she held a relator does not occupy a continuing position and therefore is not an officer of the United States who must be presidentially appointed — a relator cannot be replaced by another relator the way a special prosecutor can — relying on the Eleventh Circuit's 2026 decision in *Zafirov*. On the Vesting and Take Care Clauses, she pointed to the long history of qui tam actions before and after the founding and to the control the government keeps even when it declines to intervene: it can intervene later for good cause, move to dismiss over the relator's objection, veto a voluntary dismissal, approve or block a settlement, and stay discovery that would interfere with a criminal or civil matter.

Contrast with the Fifth Circuit: on November 13, 2026, the Fifth Circuit affirmed the dismissal of a qui tam case in *United States ex rel. Jackson v. Ventavia Research Group*, but Judge Willett wrote a lengthy concurrence raising a "serious constitutional pause" about qui tam under Article II, and Chief Judge Elrod said the concerns were "no doubt en banc worthy." Four Fifth Circuit judges have now raised the question. The practical takeaway for relator-side attorneys: expect the Article II motion in every declined qui tam case where the defendant has the resources to bring it — it is now a standard defense play, not a fringe argument. Have the answer ready before filing, with *Zafirov*, the history of qui tam, and the government's retained control mechanisms in the brief from the start. For defense counsel, the honest assessment is that the argument is gaining attention in the Fifth Circuit but has not won a single court over yet. The Supreme Court may eventually have to resolve it, but for now the scoreboard favors the relators.

## Tech angle

**Supply-chain forensics: in origin-fraud cases, the boxes, the shipping records, and the emails are the evidence.** Neither of today's cases turns on a laboratory test or a confession. In the Philips case, the proof is the paper trail around the product: the boxes that arrived marked "Made in China," the shipping records, the distributor's invoices, the contract certifications that the products were made in the United States or a designated country, and the internal emails warning the sales team not to let the VA see the packaging. China is not a designated country under the Trade Agreements Act, so the whole question becomes whether ten to fifteen seconds of hand assembly substantially transforms a Chinese mask into an American product. The same theme runs through the YDH case: counterfeit postage is detected by following the labels, the shipping volumes, and the money — the Postal Inspection Service and Customs and Border Protection worked that case together, and the labels themselves were the evidence. For anyone advising a federal contractor, country of origin is not a marketing claim. It is a certification, tested against the shipping record, the factory record, and the packaging.
