## Case of the day

**Three Department of Energy contractors settle PPP "double-dip" FCA allegations (announced Nov 16, 2026, E.D. Tenn.).** Firewater Investigations, LLC agreed to pay $405,186; Lancaster Services, Inc. agreed to pay $475,000; and Manhattan Legacy Services, LLC agreed to pay $125,000 — a combined $1,005,186. The companies held federal contracts directly or indirectly through the U.S. Department of Energy. According to the United States, each company fraudulently certified that it incurred particular payroll costs in Paycheck Protection Program loan forgiveness applications submitted to the Small Business Administration, which were approved — but the companies had already billed those same payroll costs to the Department of Energy under existing contracts and were paid for them directly or indirectly, so each contractor did not actually sustain the payroll costs certified. The investigation was conducted by the Department of Energy Office of Inspector General, Knoxville Field Office. U.S. Attorney Francis M. Hamilton III said the settlements show his office is using all tools available to redress actions that deceptively avoid financial obligations to the United States; Assistant Inspector General for Investigations Lewe Sessions said companies already receiving payroll reimbursement under federal contracts should not have claimed those expenses on forgiveness applications. The legal theory: a forgiveness application is a claim on the government, and certifying costs not actually borne makes the certification false; the False Claims Act allows treble damages plus per-claim penalties, so exposure can far exceed the loan amount. The claims are allegations only; there has been no determination of liability. No qui tam relator or whistleblower share was announced — this case came from the government's own investigators.

## FCA practice note

**Eleventh Circuit summarily reverses an Appointments Clause dismissal — Zafirov is now doing real work.** On November 12, 2026, the Eleventh Circuit granted the relators' and the government's motion for summary reversal in *Brent Berry v. Native American Services Corp.* (M.D. Fla.), vacating a district court judgment on the pleadings that had held the False Claims Act's qui tam provisions unconstitutional under the Appointments Clause. The panel (Circuit Judges Rosenbaum, Abudu, and Tjoflat) ruled in one sentence: "Because our decision in Zafirov controls the outcome of this appeal, the Relators' and the Government's motion for summary reversal is GRANTED." The relators are Dennie Gose, Sean Gose (personal representative of Dennie Gose's estate), and Brent Berry; the defendants are Native American Services Corp. and Great American Insurance Group, Inc. The case returns to the district court, which must still address the defendants' Take Care Clause and Vesting Clause arguments — the same arguments *Zafirov* (Sept 1, 2026) sent back down. Five federal appeals courts have now rejected Appointments Clause challenges to qui tam: the Fifth, Sixth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits. Takeaways: (1) the Appointments Clause attack on qui tam is a dead end in the Eleventh Circuit and will be reversed summarily, without full briefing — a summary reversal is decided on the papers because binding precedent already answers the question; (2) defendants should not build strategy on that argument; (3) relators facing such a dismissal have a strong appellate position; (4) the constitutional fight is not over — Take Care and Vesting Clause arguments remain live in the district courts.

## Tech angle

**How pandemic loan fraud gets found: the SBA's four-step machine, Palantir, and the data miners.** The Government Accountability Office's March 2025 report (GAO-25-107267) describes the Small Business Administration's antifraud process for its pandemic loan programs in four steps: (1) screening — automated review comparing each application against public and private databases and checking for internal inconsistencies; (2) data analytics — tools, sometimes using machine learning, flagging files with data anomalies for review; (3) human-led review of flagged files; (4) referral of likely fraudulent applications to the SBA Office of Inspector General. The machinery is growing: on July 14, 2026, the SBA announced it is expanding its use of Palantir software, formalizing the Fraud Prevention Pilot Program launched earlier in 2026, using advanced technology and artificial intelligence to surface data and leads, support criminal enforcement, and assist in recovering funds. The agency has announced suspensions of more than 150,000 pandemic borrowers in five states, tied to more than $10 billion in suspected fraud. On the relator side, the Justice Department's FOCUS initiative (Fraud Oversight through Careful Use of Statistics, announced April 30, 2026) invites data miners — entities that analyze public government datasets for signs of fraud without insider access — to present their tools and data signals to the Civil Fraud Section. Data miners have accounted for more than 45% of all qui tam filings since fiscal year 2024, and more than 780 qui tam complaints have already been filed in fiscal year 2026. The caution for data-miner relators: FOCUS rewards rigor and pre-filing diligence, and fraud allegations still face the heightened pleading standard (Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 9(b)) — a statistical anomaly alone is not a complaint. Context: in fiscal year 2025 the Justice Department announced more than 200 pandemic-related False Claims Act settlements and judgments exceeding $230 million. The Knoxville double-dip was caught because the same dollars showed up in two datasets — loan forgiveness applications and contract payment records — and those comparisons are only getting smarter.
