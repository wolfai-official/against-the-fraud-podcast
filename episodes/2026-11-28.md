## Case of the day

**A Florida doctor and three staff members are convicted of faking asthma drug trial data (DOJ announced Nov 23, 2026, S.D. Fla.).** A federal jury convicted Dr. Jaynier Moya, 50, co-owner of Pines Care Research Center LLC in Pembroke Pines and principal investigator for its studies, and clinical research coordinators Luis Montano, 56, and Yuniarka Garcia, 41, of wire fraud and conspiracy to commit wire fraud; coordinator Luis Cuza, 42, was convicted of conspiracy to commit wire fraud. A fifth employee, Alexandra Olivera, 38, pleaded guilty to conspiracy in August 2026. Beginning no later than 2019, the defendants fabricated testing data in clinical trials of prospective asthma treatments for an unnamed pharmaceutical sponsor: they used identification documents belonging to people who never participated to create false records showing those people were research subjects, falsified records to make it appear the purported subjects had taken the study medication and completed protocol testing, paid other individuals to undergo the tests, and attributed that data to the purported subjects. They then submitted the fabricated data to the sponsor to obtain payment for research that had not been carried out. Sentencing is set for February 9, 2027 for the four convicted at trial (Moya, Montano and Garcia each face up to 80 years; Cuza up to 20 years); Olivera is scheduled to be sentenced December 15, 2026. A federal judge will determine the actual sentences under the Sentencing Guidelines. The case was investigated by the FDA Office of Criminal Investigations, Miami Field Office, and first became public on June 10, 2026, when the Justice Department announced the indictment. These are jury convictions, not allegations. Local reporting notes the verdict is the latest in a run of federal prosecutions over faked drug-trial data at small South Florida research sites — a pattern that matters well beyond Broward County, because regulators rely on that data to decide which medicines reach pharmacy shelves.

## FCA practice note

**The Justice Department's new Super Intelligence Task Force centralizes AI enforcement — no new offense, but new visibility and guidance coming.** On November 5, 2026, the Department of Justice announced the formation of the Super Intelligence Task Force (DOJ-SITF), established by an October 13 internal memorandum and led by Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward and Associate Deputy Attorney General Stephen Weiss. It follows the president's September 29, 2026 executive order, "Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence," directing the executive branch to use the term "Super Intelligence" in place of "Artificial Intelligence." The task force absorbs the Artificial Intelligence Litigation Task Force set up in January 2026. Its remit: organize the department's criminal and civil enforcement efforts involving advanced AI, deconflict investigations and cases among DOJ components and U.S. Attorneys' Offices, recommend enforcement priorities, serve as the department's point of contact with the White House task force and other federal agencies, coordinate affirmative civil litigation involving the technology, and develop guidance on prosecuting unlawful conduct and evaluating compliance measures. Components must notify the task force when opening an investigation and before seeking charges or finalizing a resolution, though notification should not delay action in exigent circumstances. Neither the announcement nor the memorandum names a new offense or a specific statute, and the attorney general has said he will not investigate or prosecute AI companies when no statute is violated — so enforcement runs through existing laws. For False Claims Act lawyers, the exposure points are a company's use of AI in connection with government payment claims, cybersecurity certifications, and representations made while negotiating and performing government contracts; the compliance programs clients build around AI use will eventually be judged against published guidance. Watch item: the executive order's sixty-day deadline for the White House science adviser to submit proposed legislative language for a federal definition of the term falls today, November 28, 2026.

## Tech angle

**How fabricated clinical trial data gets caught: follow the paper trail.** A clinical trial is a chain of records — enrollment, visits, dosing, lab results, payment — and every link has to match the others. The Pines Care scheme broke the chain at the first link, because the named subjects were not the people who were tested. Paying real people to sit for tests generates real results, but those results belong to the wrong bodies; the moment an investigator compares identity documents to the people who actually walked into the clinic, or test results to the medical histories of the named subjects, the mismatch appears. The FDA's criminal investigators did not need to second-guess the science — they followed the records, and the records did not add up. The same data-integrity theme appeared earlier this week in the Fifth Circuit's Ventavia decision (covered November 24), where the government moved to dismiss a relator's suit challenging Pfizer's COVID vaccine trial integrity; there the dispute was about whether the government could end a private suit, while here the government brought its own criminal case and a jury convicted. Takeaways: identity verification at enrollment is the control that makes every later record trustworthy; source-document verification — matching the test result to the person who actually produced it — is the audit step that catches this kind of fraud; and the payment trail is evidence, because the defendants submitted fabricated data to get paid, so the sponsor's own payment records became part of the proof. Zooming out, the Pines Care verdict shows the government prosecuting fabricated data the old-fashioned way, while the Super Intelligence Task Force shows it organizing for a world in which the data — and the fraud — are increasingly produced by machines.
