## Case of the day

**Honeywell Aerospace Inc. — a $2,042,518 cyber-FCA settlement from September is back in court as a shareholder lawsuit (DOJ settlement announced Sept 1, 2026, W.D.N.C.; securities class action filed in D. Ariz., lead plaintiff deadline Nov 23, 2026).** Honeywell Aerospace, which became a standalone public company on June 29, 2026 after spinning off from Honeywell International, agreed to pay $2,042,518 to resolve allegations that it violated the False Claims Act by failing to comply with cybersecurity requirements in a Department of Defense contract. The allegations covered April 2020 through December 2023: a Honeywell business unit submitted claims for payment while failing to comply with the cybersecurity controls in NIST Special Publication 800-171 on one of its networks, as the contract and the Defense Department's acquisition rules (DFARS 252.204-7012) required. The work involved quantum computing services for the Defense Department. The case came from whistleblower Rachel Tenney, a former Honeywell information technology employee, who filed a qui tam lawsuit in 2022 (*United States ex rel. Rachel Tenney v. Honeywell International Inc.*, No. 3:22-cv-129 (W.D.N.C.)); the government intervened in part on August 18, 2026, covering the cybersecurity allegations, and declined the rest. Tenney receives $375,823 as her share, about 18 percent of the total. According to the complaint, Tenney raised the cybersecurity concerns internally and was pushed out; she alleged the company relied on a firewall to protect the network carrying the quantum computing work — a firewall she described as a "smokescreen" hiding inadequate security practices, with weak malicious code protection, weak access controls, and inadequate incident testing and response. Honeywell did not admit the allegations; the claims are allegations only, with no determination of liability and no finding of a hack or breach. Second act: a securities class action was filed in federal court in Arizona under Sections 10(b) and 20(a) of the Exchange Act and Rule 10b-5, naming the company and its CEO James Currier and CFO Joshua Jepsen individually. The class period runs June 29–September 1, 2026, from the spin-off to the day DOJ announced the settlement. The complaint alleges the company concealed that a small group of suppliers carried a disproportionate share of sales and faced supply constraints, and that the company was under investigation for potential False Claims Act violations tied to cybersecurity requirements in a Defense Department contract — while its filings warned only in general terms that FCA enforcement "could" happen. Two corrective events: on August 6, 2026, after second-quarter results and a cut to the full-year forecast, the stock fell $47.17 (23.16%) to close at $156.47; on September 1, 2026, the day DOJ announced the settlement, it fell another $3.87 (2.45%) to $154.24. The deadline to apply to lead the case is Monday, November 23, 2026. Attorney takeaway: a False Claims Act settlement does not end the exposure — it creates a public record, a date, and a dollar figure that securities plaintiffs can build a case around, and the government's press release is the event the market trades on.

## FCA practice note

**DOJ rewrote its own False Claims Act playbook: guidance-only enforcement is out, and every declined qui tam now gets a dismissal assessment.** On September 18, 2026, the Justice Department announced two revisions to the Justice Manual. First, Section 1-19.000, now titled "Limitations on Issuance and Use of Guidance Documents," provides that enforcement actions must rest on violations of applicable legal requirements, not on noncompliance with guidance documents alone, and that the department may not bring an action based solely on allegations that a company ignored agency guidance. The change revives the 2017–2018 Sessions and Brand memoranda, reversing the approach taken under Attorney General Merrick Garland. But the word "solely" matters: guidance can still show what a company knew, show industry standards, explain technical processes, and — most important for contractors — where a contract requires compliance with agency guidance, it is the contract, not the guidance itself, that makes the guidance pertinent. A case built only on a Medicare manual or an FAQ page now has a defense argument the manual itself supports; a case built on a contract that incorporates those standards by reference does not. Second, Section 4-4.111 now requires DOJ attorneys, when declining to intervene in a qui tam case, to assess in every case whether the government should move to dismiss under 31 U.S.C. § 3730(c)(2)(A) — up from the old "should also consider" language. The first of the seven Granston dismissal factors was widened from "curbing meritless qui tams that facially lack merit" to simply "curbing meritless qui tams," broadening the set of cases eligible for dismissal, and DOJ says it may re-evaluate dismissal as litigation progresses. The manual cautions that dismissal will not be warranted in every declined case, since the government often investigates only far enough to decide declination is appropriate. The revisions land the same week the Fifth Circuit affirmed the government's dismissal of the Brook Jackson qui tam over Pfizer vaccine trials under the Supreme Court's Polansky decision (Nov 13, 2026). Practical points: for relators, a declination letter now comes with a dismissal decision attached, so build the record of merit early and expect a Granston motion; for defense counsel, a theory that begins and ends with guidance is now a live argument, but contract-incorporated standards survive — and the FCA limitations period can reach back ten years, so a record created under one administration's policy can be judged under another's.

## Tech angle

**How the fraud was proved twice: an insider's network map and a stock chart.** The Honeywell whistleblower was an information technology employee, and her complaint reads like an audit. The network carrying the quantum computing work for the Defense Department was supposed to meet the 110 security controls in NIST SP 800-171, the standard the Defense Department's acquisition rule (DFARS 252.204-7012) requires for systems holding covered defense information. What she alleged was not a breach but a gap between the paper and the practice: a firewall presented as protection, described as a "smokescreen"; inadequate malicious code protection; weak access control; incident testing and response not up to standard; oversight of suspicious activity falling short. Honeywell is the third case in that pattern in just over two months — ILC Dover agreed to pay $13 million on November 3, 2026 and Johnson Controls agreed to pay $5 million on November 13, 2026, both over alleged failures to meet the same kind of cybersecurity requirements in federal contracts. In each case the proof was the company's own compliance record: network diagrams, firewall configurations, control assessments, and the internal complaints the whistleblower raised before leaving. For attorneys, that is the discovery list — those documents are the case whether or not anyone was ever attacked. The second detection layer is the market: the securities complaint is built on disclosure forensics — two corrective stock drops and a class period running from the spin-off to the DOJ announcement — with plaintiffs' firms mining DOJ press releases and SEC filings for the gap between what a company said and what it knew. The whistleblower found the gap inside the network; the market found the gap in the disclosures; both findings came from records the company itself created.

**Docket note:** The Commodity Futures Trading Commission published a notice on November 16, 2026 asking to extend its whistleblower information collection — the forms for tips (Form TCR) and award applications (Form WB-APP) — with public comments due January 15, 2027. The Commission estimates about 1,800 people a year use those forms, at about half an hour each. Routine paperwork, but also a reminder that the tip pipeline itself is a system the government has to maintain.
