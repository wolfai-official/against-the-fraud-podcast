## Case of the day

**The Justice Department announces its largest-ever anti-scam crackdown: 800+ defendants, 400+ cases, $8 billion-plus in intended losses (Nov 24, 2026).** At a press conference in Washington, Attorney General Todd Blanche, FBI Director Kash Patel, and Criminal Division Assistant Attorney General A. Tysen Duva announced the results of an enforcement surge following President Trump's Executive Order 14390, signed March 6, 2026, directing a government-wide crackdown on scams. Since the order, Justice Department prosecutors have taken action in more than 400 cases against more than 800 defendants in 85 federal districts, with 30 defendants extradited to face charges in U.S. courts. Officials said the department has recovered more than $8 billion in intended victim losses. Gallup reports that 10% of U.S. adults experienced a scam last year and that Americans lost an estimated $68 billion to scams in 2025.

The centerpiece is the FBI's Operation Blackout, a global scam-center demolition initiative: nearly $17 billion in illicit funds seized or restrained, more than 14,000 digital devices taken, and more than 29 scam compounds dismantled across Asia, Africa, and the Middle East. More than 50 scam actors were charged federally, foreign partners arrested or detained more than 320 additional perpetrators overseas, and the FBI helped liberate more than 2,200 trafficked workers forced to operate inside the compounds. Operation Level Up, the prevention arm, has prevented more than $143 million in victim losses since March 6, warned thousands of Americans they were being targeted, and frozen more than $350 million in assets through the FBI's Recovery Asset Team.

The schemes charged cover nearly every scam Americans meet: Southeast Asian cryptocurrency investment fraud centers, Nigerian inheritance-fee fraud, a Mexican list broker who knowingly sold elderly Americans' names and contact information to dozens of scammers, a Peruvian call center that told Spanish-speaking Americans they had to pay for English language-learning products or be arrested or deported, and a sweepstakes scheme defrauding elderly Americans involving a dozen defendants including several Jamaican nationals. In the Georgia prison jury-duty scam, current and former prison gang members used contraband mobile phones to tell victims they had missed jury duty and would be arrested unless they paid a fine; 14 people were charged in three indictments in federal and state court, and 17 more were charged in state court for smuggling contraband phones into Georgia prisons. The websites the inmates used were seized. On November 19, 2026, the FCC approved a rule allowing federal and state prisons and jails to use jamming technology to block calls from illegal contraband cell phones.

Other agencies in the whole-of-government surge: Homeland Security Investigations executed more than 300 arrests, secured about 80 convictions and guilty pleas, and seized more than $120 million; the Secret Service restrained more than $200 million in illicit funds and disrupted 1,780 domains operated by scammers; the FTC filed 16 actions and says it has returned almost $3 billion to American consumers harmed by fraud; the SEC charged 39 entities for allegedly filing false Forms ADV to pose as legitimate investment advisers; and the State Department helped freeze more than $1 billion in stolen cryptocurrency and supported the takedown of more than 1.4 million fraudulent websites and accounts.

Two cautions: an indictment is merely an allegation, and every defendant is presumed innocent until proven guilty beyond a reasonable doubt. And these are criminal cases, not whistleblower settlements — but the pipeline is the same one this show follows. Tips and insiders who report what they see are how many of these investigations start, and the agencies in the surge run tip lines and whistleblower programs.

**Second story: AcuMedGroup agrees to pay $500,000 to settle False Claims Act allegations over inflated VA acupuncture billing (Nov 24, 2026).** AcuMedGroup LLC and its owner, Cecilia Rusnak, agreed to pay $500,000 to resolve allegations that they submitted claims to the Department of Veterans Affairs for acupuncture services that were inflated and did not meet the minimum time requirements for payment by the VA. The claims were allegedly submitted between February 24, 2016 and March 10, 2022. The settlement resulted from a coordinated effort by the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Middle District of Florida and the VA Office of Inspector General. The claims resolved are allegations only, with no determination of liability. Time-based billing — charging for more minutes than the service took — is one of the easiest patterns for an auditor to spot, because the math does not need a witness. It needs a clock.

## FCA practice note

**The First Circuit revives a PPP qui tam suit against private equity firms — the public disclosure bar is not triggered just because some pieces were public.** On November 24, 2026, the First Circuit reversed a Rhode Island district court that had thrown out a whistleblower's False Claims Act suit against New Harbor Capital Fund LP and other private equity firms over Paycheck Protection Program loans. The case is *United States ex rel. James R. Berkley v. Ocean State, LLC*, C.A. No. 20-cv-538 (D.R.I.). Berkley alleged that companies that received PPP loans made misrepresentations to get them, and that the New Harbor private equity firms were involved; the New Harbor entities themselves did not apply for or receive program loans — they are the investment firms and the management company behind the companies that did.

Chief Judge John J. McConnell, Jr. granted summary judgment for the defendants on September 26, 2025, holding that the suit was barred by the FCA's public disclosure bar, which blocks a qui tam suit when its allegations are substantially similar to information already publicly disclosed, unless the relator is an original source of the information. Judge Seth R. Aframe, writing for the First Circuit, held that the district court erred in concluding the suit failed under the bar. The case goes back, and the defendants will have to defend the allegations on the merits.

Berkley's dispute with Ocean State started as a landlord-tenant fight. To pressure Ocean State to settle, he began investigating its PPP loan applications, reading publicly available information and speaking with Ocean State employees. He filed the qui tam suit, amended it twice, and the case survived a motion to dismiss before being ended on summary judgment.

The practice question is when public information kills a qui tam case and when it does not. The First Circuit's answer, at least on these facts, is that the bar is not triggered just because some of the pieces were public — the court asks whether the relator's allegations are substantially similar to what was actually disclosed, and a relator who did his own investigation and brought non-public detail can survive. Compare *United States ex rel. Relator LLC v. Howard D. Kootstra and Golden Empire Mortgage, Inc.*, No. 1:22-cv-00924-TLN-CDB (E.D. Cal., Aug 5, 2024), where a relator built a complaint almost entirely from data pulled from the government's PandemicOversight.gov website, including the defendant's industry code and the fact that it received a PPP loan; that court dismissed the case, holding that the relator added nothing the public record did not already show.

The two decisions together draw the line: a relator who repackages a public database is likely barred; a relator who investigates, talks to insiders, and adds facts that were not in the public record has a real chance, even if the starting point was public information. Pandemic-era qui tam cases are still being litigated years after the loans were made, and the public disclosure bar remains one of the first defenses a defendant reaches for. Do not assume a public database ends the case. And do not assume it saves it, either.

## Tech angle

**How fake mortgage documents get caught: verify against the source.** Two guilty pleas announced by the Justice Department's Middle District of Florida on November 23 and 24, 2026 show the pattern.

Richard Exposito, 64, of North Bay Village, a licensed mortgage loan officer, pleaded guilty on November 23 to conspiracy to commit bank fraud. From December 2016 to July 2019, he conspired with document preparer Kiomy Torres and others to submit mortgage applications with false supporting documents to the financial institution where he worked. Exposito received referrals from Torres, told her the income and asset amounts the referrals needed to qualify, and she supplied false pay stubs, pension statements, and disability payment statements to hit those numbers. Exposito then prepared and submitted the applications and received commissions when they were approved. He faces a maximum of 30 years in prison. Torres pleaded guilty on May 5, 2026 and is scheduled to be sentenced on December 16, 2026 by Judge Carlos E. Mendoza. The case was investigated by the Federal Housing Finance Agency OIG, the HUD OIG, and the FBI.

Desean Ford, 55, a licensed mortgage broker in Jacksonville, pleaded guilty on November 24 to making a false statement to influence a mortgage lending business. In 2021, Ford applied for a VA-guaranteed mortgage loan and listed a commercial cleaning company as his employer. He submitted altered bank statements that inflated his checking and savings balances by more than $90,000 and included fictitious payroll deposits from the cleaning company, plus what appeared to be pay stubs and W-2 forms from the same company. When investigators subpoenaed the payroll company that supposedly issued the pay stubs, it had no record of managing payroll or preparing pay stubs for that cleaning company — and the pay stubs Ford supplied listed the wrong address for the company. The loan had been funded at $245,520. Ford agreed to forfeit that full amount and faces a maximum of 30 years in prison.

The lesson applies far beyond mortgages: a forged document is only convincing until someone checks it against the source. The payroll company had the records, the bank had the real balances, and the cleaning company's real address was public. The same logic runs through the scam crackdown: the Postal Inspection Service seized the domains the Georgia prison inmates used for their jury-duty scheme, and investigators froze more than $1 billion in cryptocurrency by following the money through wallets and exchanges, not by taking anyone's word for it.

For attorneys, the through line is that the strongest qui tam case is not the one with the angriest allegations — it is the one where the documents can be verified against an independent source, the way the payroll company's records showed Ford's pay stubs were fake. One caution: verification often catches fraud only after the money moves. The Ford loan was already funded when the fraud was caught. A person inside the room can stop a fraud before the checks are cut; a database, and a subpoena, can only catch it after.
