## Case of the day

**InnovAge Holding Corp. agrees to pay $36 million in the first-ever PACE False Claims Act settlement (announced Nov 17, 2026, D. Colo.).** InnovAge, a Denver-based operator of PACE centers in California, Colorado, New Mexico, and Virginia, agreed to pay approximately $25.7 million to the United States and a combined $10.3 million to the four states to resolve allegations that it violated the federal False Claims Act and state false claims laws. PACE — the Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly — is a dual Medicare-Medicaid program for people 55 and older who qualify for nursing-home-level care but can live at home with support; the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) pays the PACE organization a fixed monthly amount for each enrolled beneficiary, and in return the organization must provide comprehensive health care services as expeditiously as the participant's health condition requires. The settlement resolves allegations that, from May 2016 to October 2022, InnovAge failed to maintain sufficient specialist provider networks and, in thousands of instances, failed to timely schedule appointments or consultations for specialist medical care, including oncology, cardiology, surgery, and neurology. According to the Justice Department, InnovAge's internal reports of providers' medical orders regularly showed numerous and recurrent delinquent specialist referrals; several senior InnovAge physicians complained that the scheduling practices and delays had negative health consequences for participants, placed legal risk on the company's providers, and put the company out of compliance with CMS requirements; and the company's internal audits found significant delays in specialist care, including numerous delayed orders, many over a year outstanding. Acting Assistant Attorney General Jonathan D. Guynn called it a "historic, first-of-its-kind settlement" demonstrating the Civil Division's commitment to combating health care fraud by PACE organizations. The civil settlement resolves qui tam claims brought by two former employees, Monita Poudyal and Karen Lapcewich, in separate suits filed in 2021 and 2022 (D. Colo. Nos. 1:21-cv-02464 and 1:22-cv-01686); the relator share of the federal settlement will be $4.5 million, about 17.5% of the federal recovery. The claims are allegations only; there has been no determination of liability. Background: CMS audited InnovAge in 2021 and found it was failing to provide medically necessary services, suspending new enrollment at the Sacramento center in September 2021 and at the Colorado centers effective December 23, 2021; both sanctions were eventually lifted after the company passed validation audits.

## FCA practice note

**The capitated-care FCA theory: the false claim is the monthly payment, not an inflated invoice.** In a fee-for-service case the government says the provider billed for something it did not do. In InnovAge, the theory is different: the provider collected a fixed monthly payment for a person and then did not deliver the care that payment was buying. That theory reaches beyond PACE — managed care plans, Medicare Advantage organizations, and any provider paid a flat amount per patient face the same logic, and the evidence will look like the evidence here: referral backlogs, network-adequacy gaps, internal reports showing orders sitting for months, and clinicians warning management in writing. Attorneys advising capitated providers should ask whether their clients can prove, with data, that referrals are completed on time, because here the company's own records did most of the government's work. Two further practice points: (1) the settlement announcement mentions no corporate integrity agreement — many large health care settlements come with one, so do not assume a settlement of this size automatically carries one; read the announcement and the agreement when it is public; (2) relator math — two relators, two separate lawsuits, one coordinated settlement, and a single combined share of $4.5 million; when multiple relators file on the same conduct, the government can resolve the cases together and the share is negotiated as one number, so a second filer's recovery may depend on how the cases are coordinated, not just on what that relator knew.

## Tech angle

**How the fraud was proved: the provider's own data, accumulated over years.** There was no dramatic raid and no single smoking-gun document. The proof was InnovAge's own referral-timeliness data: internal reports tracking delinquent referrals, internal audits measuring how long specialist orders sat outstanding, and a federal audit in 2021 that reached the same conclusions independently. In capitated care the fraud is not hidden in a billing code — it is visible in a queue, in the time between a doctor ordering a specialist visit and the visit actually happening. The same pattern runs through the show's earlier cases: cyber fraud in filed security scores and certifications, lab fraud in payment records matched against referral data. For attorneys and compliance officers, the practical lesson is to build the referral-timeliness dashboard before the government builds the case from it — and to know that if the system can produce that report and the numbers are bad, the report is discoverable.

**AI-whistleblower watch:** On November 17, 2026, Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield sued OpenAI in Multnomah County Circuit Court, seeking at least $2.035 billion in damages under Oregon's Unlawful Trade Practices Act. The 53-page complaint alleges OpenAI marketed ChatGPT as safe for children while knowing it lacks adequate safeguards, engineered the chatbot to be overly affirming and foster emotional dependence, and failed to confirm parental consent for users as young as 13. The state asks the court to order stronger safeguards, including effective age checks, protections against harmful interactions, accurate risk disclosures, and reliable parental alerts; testing cited in the complaint found missing or delayed parental alerts during discussions of self-harm and eating disorders. Rayfield announced the suit as part of a new Keeping Kids Safe Initiative and said no specific incident in Oregon prompted the filing. Oregon is the ninth state to sue OpenAI over ChatGPT's safety for minors; eight other states filed similar suits in October. The House Democrats' letter to OpenAI led by Rep. Greg Casar (Oct 29, 2026), asking for answers to 11 questions about the firing of three safety researchers and security incidents involving the company's models, had a November 13 deadline; as of this morning no public response has been found.
